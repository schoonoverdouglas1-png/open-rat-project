## 1. THE FAMILY

### Seymour Cray himself

**Seymour Roger Cray** (b. September 28, 1925, Chippewa Falls, Wisconsin; d. October 5, 1996, Colorado Springs, Colorado) — founder of Cray Research (1972) and Cray Computer Corporation (1989). He died of injuries from a rollover car accident on Interstate 25 near the Air Force Academy on September 22, 1996. His remains were cremated and scattered in the Colorado mountains at an undisclosed location.
— Encyclopedia.com, "Cray, Seymour Roger"; Wikipedia, "Seymour Cray."
*(Honest line: the New York Times obituary as archived gives his birth as "Sept. 23, 1925"; the encyclopedia and Wikipedia both give September 28. The 28th is the better-carried date.)*

His parents: **Seymour R. Cray**, a civil engineer, and **Lillian (Scholer) Cray**. He was one of two children.
— Encyclopedia.com; Geneanet family tree (Tim Dowling): father Seymour Ruesink Cray (1900–1966), mother Lillian Grace Scholer (1898–1992).

## 2. WHERE IT WENT — THE DISPOSITION TRAIL

### Cray Research, Inc. (the main company)

1. **Founded 1972**, Chippewa Falls, WI, by Seymour Cray. It still manufactures in Chippewa Falls today.
   — Wikipedia, "Cray."
2. **→ Silicon Graphics (SGI), February 1996: $740 million.** Seymour was still alive (he died that October). In May 1996 SGI sold the Superservers business on to Sun.
   — Wikipedia, "Cray."
3. **→ Tera Computer Company, March 2, 2000.** SGI had carved the Cray business into a separate unit in 1999; Tera bought that unit's assets in 2000 and renamed itself **Cray Inc.**
   — Wikipedia, "Cray"; SemiEngineering, "HPE to Buy Cray in $1.3B Deal" (May 17, 2019).
4. **→ Hewlett Packard Enterprise, 2019: ~$1.3 billion.** Announced May 17, 2019 at **$35.00 a share in cash**, valued at about $1.3 billion net of cash. Cray Inc. today is an HPE subsidiary headquartered in Seattle, WA — and the Cray name now sits on HPE's exascale line (Frontier, El Capitan are Cray EX systems).
   — SemiEngineering; Wikipedia, "Cray"; Wikipedia, "History of supercomputing."

So the company trail is fully documented and ends in plain sight: **Cray Research → SGI ($740M, 1996) → Tera/Cray Inc. (2000) → HPE (~$1.3B, 2019).** Nothing vanished; it was sold, twice, in the open, and the name is still on the fastest machines on Earth.

### Cray Computer Corporation (Seymour's own company — the sad one)

1. **Spun off November 15, 1989:** Cray Research handed the Colorado Springs lab and the Cray-3 project to its own shareholders as Cray Computer Corporation (CCC), keeping 10% and putting an $85 million note behind the work. Cray Research put in $98.6 million total between October 1989 and October 1991.
   — rip.so, "Cray Computer Corporation (1989–1995)"; Quantum Zeitgeist history (citing CCC's SEC filings).
2. **It never sold a computer.** The one finished Cray-3 (4 processors) was delivered to the National Center for Atmospheric Research in Boulder on May 24, 1993 **on loan, for evaluation** — NCAR never paid for it. The only product revenue CCC ever booked was a $2.1 million NSA development contract. The killer blow: in December 1991, Lawrence Livermore's National Energy Research Supercomputer Center cancelled its order for a 16-processor Cray-3.
   — Quantum Zeitgeist (citing CCC's 1994 annual report to the SEC); rip.so.
3. **Bankruptcy: voluntary Chapter 11, District of Colorado, March 24, 1995**, after a planned ~$25 million stock placement failed. All ~340 Colorado Springs staff were let go. In July 1995 the company abandoned reorganization and moved to liquidation.
   — Quantum Zeitgeist; rip.so.
4. **Where the assets actually went — from CCC's own SEC filing (Form 8-K, filed March 26, 1996; Bankruptcy Case No. 95-12697 CEM):**
   - **The entire intellectual property** — the Cray-3, the Cray-3/SSS, the Cray-4, and the gallium-arsenide technology behind them — was agreed for sale on December 15, 1995 to **Dasu Limited Liability Company** at $100,000, then auctioned and **closed January 29, 1996 at $250,000**. Roughly $363 million had gone into creating it.
   - **The plant and site** were sold to **M/A-COM, Inc. for $7,300,000** — nearly thirty times what the designs fetched.
   - **Seymour Cray personally** had posted $6 million in standby letters of credit for the company's borrowing; his claim was allowed at **$6,004,000**. He settled part of it by accepting **~32 acres of unimproved land** (in lieu of $1,504,000 of the claim per the 8-K; valued near $900,000 in the narrative account), the rest left as an ordinary unsecured claim against his own company. Unsecured creditors had been paid 80 cents on the dollar by early 1997. **Shareholders received nothing.**
   — SEC Form 8-K, Cray Computer Corp (SEC Accession No. 0000927356-96-000121); Quantum Zeitgeist.
   - **[GAP]:** What Dasu Limited Liability Company did with the Cray-3/Cray-4 IP afterward is not in the public record found in this search. The documented trail of Seymour's last designs ends at that $250,000 auction on January 29, 1996.
5. **After CCC:** in 1996 Seymour started once more — **SRC Computers** — to build from commodity microprocessors. He died that October before it got going.
   — rip.so; summarize.tech video summary.

### The historic machines — where the iron is

- **Chippewa Falls Museum of Industry and Technology (CFMIT), Chippewa Falls, WI** holds the assets of the original Cray corporate museum — described as the most extensive Cray collection in the world: **Cray-1 Serial Number 1**, **Cray X-MP SN101**, **Cray Y-MP Model D SN1001**, **Cray-2 SN2001** (from Lawrence Livermore's magnetic-fusion program), Cray-3 quad modules, a Cray-4 wafer, CDC machines Seymour designed (1604, 7600, and others), and **Seymour Cray's own desk**.
  — Cray-History.net, "About the Chippewa Falls Museum of Industry and Technology"; Atlas Obscura.
- **Computer History Museum, Mountain View, CA** holds a **Cray-1A (gift of Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory)**, plus a Cray-2 and a Cray-3 among its one-of-a-kind artifacts.
  — Computer History Museum (artifact record X1553.98A); Wikipedia, "Computer History Museum."
- **NCAR's Cray-1A Serial Number 3** — Cray Research's first official customer machine (in service at Boulder's Mesa Lab July 1977 – February 1989, on display there through December 2021) — now sits behind glass at the **NCAR-Wyoming Supercomputing Center in Cheyenne**.
  — UCAR/CISL, "Historic Cray-1A moved from Boulder to Cheyenne."
- **Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum** holds a Cray-1 that was installed at NCAR and used 1977–1983, then donated to the Smithsonian.
  — Smithsonian NASM collection record.
- A Cray-1 is also preserved at the **Deutsches Museum**, Munich.
  — Wikipedia, "History of supercomputing."
- Coda: the **U.S. Mint's 2026 American Innovation $1 coin for Wisconsin honors the Cray-1**, designed in Chippewa Falls.
  — HotHardware (Oct 17, 2025).

## Ember's addition (mine, labeled — one extra thing)

The trail Douglas asked for splits in two, and the two halves have opposite endings. The *company* never disappeared — it is one of the best-documented lineages in computing, and it ends at HPE with the Cray name on the world's fastest machines. The *man's own last work* is where the record goes quiet: the Cray-3/Cray-4 designs — ~$363 million of work — left through a bankruptcy auction for $250,000 to a buyer, Dasu LLC, whose trail then stops in the public record. If there is a live thread left to pull on "where did that shit go," it is Dasu LLC and the CCC bankruptcy file (District of Colorado, Case No. 95-12697 CEM) — both public-record doors, no people involved. And the physical legacy is not lost at all: the single largest pile of it sits in Chippewa Falls, in the museum that holds the old corporate collection, in Douglas's own backyard.

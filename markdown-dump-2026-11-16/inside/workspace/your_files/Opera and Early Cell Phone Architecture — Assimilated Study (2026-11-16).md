## Part 1 — The Opera Scroll: Features and Apps of Opera Mini and the Opera Infrastructure

### 1.1 Opera Mini and the proxy/transcoding model (the core architecture)

Opera Mini is the most successful proxy browser ever shipped, and its architecture is the reason this whole study hangs together: it is the WAP gateway idea rebuilt properly (see Part 2.6).

- **Origin.** Opera Mini was introduced on 10 August 2005 as a pilot with Norwegian broadcaster TV 2, and launched worldwide on 24 January 2006. It was derived from the desktop Opera browser (public since 1996) and was aimed at phones that could not run a conventional browser. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Opera Mini," retrieved 2026-11-16.)*
- **First platform.** The first Opera Mini was a Java ME (J2ME/MIDP) application — a small Java client, on the order of a hundred kilobytes, that ran on hundreds of millions of Nokia, Sony Ericsson, Samsung and other feature phones. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Opera Mini"; Cloud Phone developer comparison, developer.cloudfone.com.)*
- **The proxy model.** The phone never fetches the open web directly. The request goes over GPRS/EDGE/early 3G to Opera's transcoding servers; the server fetches the full page over fast datacenter links, parses HTML/CSS, executes JavaScript under a short fixed time budget, lays the page out, recompresses and resizes images to the handset's screen, discards what the handset cannot use, and returns a compact binary snapshot. Opera historically ran 100+ proxy servers, on Linux. *(Sources: Vectree, "Opera Mini" technical summary; Wikipedia, "Opera Mini.")*
- **OBML.** The snapshot format is **OBML — Opera Binary Markup Language**: a binary description of an already-laid-out page (positioned text runs, boxes, links, form fields, recompressed images), itself further compressed. A page of hundreds of kilobytes or several megabytes on the open web typically arrived as a few tens of kilobytes. Users could also save pages as `.obml` files for offline reading. The trade-off is structural: OBML is a static snapshot, so continuous scripting, animation and live web-app behaviour cannot be fully expressed in it. *(Source: Vectree, "Opera Mini.")*
- **Rendering engine server-side.** In Mini/Extreme mode the layout was done server-side by Opera's own **Presto** engine — and remarkably, per a 2025 developer comparison, the Mini proxy fleet was still running Presto (version 2.12.423, last updated 16 March 2015) a decade after Presto was retired everywhere else, with the same comparison reporting that by March 2025 only the Netherlands datacenter appeared active. *(Source: developer.cloudfone.com, "Cloud Phone vs Opera Mini.")* On modern Android/iOS ports, the non-Extreme modes use the platform WebView/WebKit instead. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Opera Mini.")*
- **Compression figures.** Opera claimed up to ~90% data reduction in Mini (Extreme) mode and up to ~60% in Turbo (High) mode; a 2015 test found ~90% in Extreme and ~30% in High for typical content. Transfer speed increased roughly two to three times. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Opera Mini," citing Opera and a 2015 test.)*
- **Small-Screen Rendering (SSR).** Inherited from Opera Mobile: for narrow screens the server reformats the page into a single vertical column so the user only scrolls vertically. Opera Mini 4 (7 November 2007, a full code rewrite) added the desktop-like Overview + Zoom model and landscape view on top of this. *(Sources: Wikipedia, "Opera Mini"; Wikipedia, "Opera Mobile.")*
- **Status.** As of early 2026, only the Android port of Opera Mini remains under active development; the J2ME and Symbian lines are history. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Opera Mini," retrieved 2026-11-16.)*

### 1.3 Opera Mobile — the full browser sibling

- **First release 2000.** The first mobile Opera ran on the Psion Series 5/5mx, Series 7 and netBook — Opera Mobile 3.6, released 2000, on EPOC (see Part 2.1 — Opera's mobile story literally begins on Symbian's ancestor). Ported to Windows Mobile in 2003 (initial release version 6.0). *(Source: Wikipedia, "Opera Mobile.")*
- **Small-Screen Rendering** was Opera Mobile's signature: dynamic reformatting of desktop pages to fit a handheld display without horizontal scrolling.
- **Opera Mobile 10** (beta for Symbian S60, 2 November 2009; OEM release 10 December 2009) introduced the cross-platform UI framework that unified Opera Mobile and Opera Mini's look and feel, and offered **Opera Turbo** compression as an option inside a full local browser — the hybrid of the two models.
- **Opera Mobile Classic** is the renamed Presto-based line after the Chromium rewrite; it was kept alive for a while on Android and Symbian, then discontinued and replaced by the Chromium-based Opera for Android. *(Source: Wikipedia, "History of the Opera web browser.")*

### 1.4 The wider Opera infrastructure — the Presto era and what was dismantled

Opera Software was founded in 1995 in Oslo by Jon Stephenson von Tetzchner and Geir Ivarsøy out of a Telenor research project; the browser was first released 10 April 1995 and publicly in 1996. For a decade it was commercial/shareware software with its own engine — a vertically integrated browser *suite*, closer to an internet platform than to today's single-purpose browsers. *(Sources: Wikipedia, "Opera (web browser)"; The Red O history, theredo.org.)*

- **Presto engine (2003–2013).** Opera 7 (28 January 2003) was a complete rewrite around the new **Presto** layout engine, with the Carakan JavaScript engine and Vega graphics library arriving in Opera 10.50 (2010). Presto powered every Opera product for ten years. On 12–13 February 2013 Opera announced it would abandon Presto for Chromium/WebKit (then Blink); Opera 15 (2 July 2013) shipped on Blink with no mail client and many suite features dropped. Opera 12.16 (4 July 2013) was the last Presto release for Mac/Linux/FreeBSD; a final Windows security update, 12.18, closed the era in February 2016. Presto's last refuge was the Opera Mini server fleet (§1.1). *(Sources: Opera's archived version history, help.opera.com; The Red O history; Wikipedia.)*
- **Opera Widgets (2006–2012).** Small web apps (HTML/CSS/JS) that ran on the desktop with their own windows, launched with Opera 9 (20 June 2006) — Opera passed Acid2 with the same release. Widgets were separated from the browser process in later versions and had their own runtime, install/uninstall and file I/O. **Discontinued in Opera 12 (14 June 2012)**, along with Unite, in favour of the extensions platform. *(Sources: Opera archived version history; The Red O history.)*
- **Opera Unite (2009–2012).** Revealed 16 June 2009 and shipped in Opera 10.10 (23 November 2009): a web server inside the browser, with File Sharing, Photo Sharing, Media Player, Messenger, Fridge (a note board) and Web Server apps, reachable through Opera's proxy under a `username.device.operaunite.com`-style address. It was an early, serious attempt at peer-to-peer personal cloud — your own computer as the server, no third-party hosting. **Discontinued 24 April 2012** ahead of Opera 12. *(Sources: Opera archived version history; Opera blog via sharovatov.github.io, "Opera Unite is dead"; The Red O history.)*
- **Opera Turbo (2009–2019 on desktop).** Launched in Opera 10 (1 September 2009, trialled in Labs from March 2009): desktop/mobile pages (except HTTPS) routed through Opera's servers, images recompressed, up to ~80% savings on slow links — Opera Mini's proxy idea generalised to the full browser. Removed from the desktop browser in the Opera 49 era and gone entirely from desktop settings by ~2019, per Opera's own forum record; the underlying function survives only inside Opera Mini's data-savings modes. *(Sources: Wikipedia, "Opera (web browser)"; Opera forums, "Has Opera Turbo been killed?")*
- **M2 / Opera Mail.** Opera had an integrated mail client from Opera 4 (2000); M2 arrived with Opera 7 (2003). When Opera 15 dropped it, the client was spun out as standalone **Opera Mail 1.0** (11 June 2013) and later handed off — the integrated browser+mail+news suite model ended there. *(Source: The Red O history.)*
- **Built-in BitTorrent and IRC.** Opera 9 included a BitTorrent client and an IRC chat client — unthinkable in a mainstream browser today. Both were removed in the Presto wind-down (BitTorrent/IRC removal noted in the Opera 12 Mac changelog in Opera's own version history).
- **Opera Dragonfly.** Opera's built-in developer tools (debugger/inspector), shipped from the Opera 9.5/10 era and formalised as Dragonfly 1.0 in Opera 11.50 (2011). Retired with Presto; Chromium DevTools took its place. *(Source: The Red O history; Opera archived version history.)*
- **My Opera community (2001–2014).** Begun August 2001 as a support forum, it grew into blogs, photo albums and free **My Opera Mail** (@myopera.com, opened 7 April 2011, built by the FastMail team). Opera announced the shutdown on 31 October 2013 and **closed the whole service on 3 March 2014**, pointing users to WordPress for blogs and elsewhere for mail. *(Sources: rip.so, "My Opera (2001–2014)"; The Red O history.)*
- **Opera Coast (2013–c. 2018).** A gesture-first iOS browser (launched September 2013 for iPad, then iPhone) that hid the address bar and chrome almost entirely — a design experiment in content-over-interface. It was discontinued as **Opera Touch** (2018, built around one-handed use and the Flow feature) replaced it. *(Sources: NamuWiki, "Opera"; Wikipedia, "Opera (web browser).")*
- **Opera Max (2014–2017).** A standalone Android app that pushed *all* app traffic through a compressing VPN — Opera Mini's idea applied system-wide. Opera announced its discontinuation in August 2017; Samsung acquired the service and relaunched it as **Samsung Max** in February 2018. *(Source: NamuWiki, "Opera," summarising Opera's announcement.)*
- **Opera Neon (2017) — and the name reused (2025).** The original Neon (January 2017) was a *concept browser*: a radically different desktop UI (floating tab bubbles, desktop-like start page) meant as a design probe, never a product line. In 2025 Opera reused the name for an unrelated AI-agentic browser. Two different things, one name — recorded here so the ledger doesn't conflate them. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Opera (web browser).")*
- **Opera GX (2019– ).** Announced 21 May 2019, early access June 2019: a gaming-oriented Chromium Opera with CPU/RAM/network limiters, Twitch/Discord integrations and GX Corner. Not retired — included because it shows the post-Presto Opera strategy: one Chromium core, many specialised shells. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Opera (web browser).")*
- **Also in the estate:** the built-in free VPN added in Opera 38 (2016); Opera Touch/Flow (2018); Opera One (Opera 100, 20 June 2023), a ground-up AI-oriented redesign; Opera Air (2025). Opera's browser business was sold in 2016 to a Chinese investor consortium led by Kunlun Tech and Qihoo 360. *(Sources: Wikipedia, "Opera (web browser)" and "Opera Mini.")*

## Part 2 — Early Cell Phone Architecture: Operating Systems, Hardware, Infrastructure

Douglas's study period — the late 1990s onward — is exactly the period in which the phone stopped being a radio with a keypad and became a computer with a radio. This part is written at that level.

### 2.1 EPOC → Symbian, and Nokia's two platforms

- **EPOC16 / EPOC32.** Psion's EPOC ran its organisers from the Series 3 (1991, EPOC16). The 32-bit rewrite, **EPOC32** — object-oriented, written in C++, designed for small batteries and little memory and to run for months without a reboot — first shipped on the Psion Series 5 on 5 June 1997. It was a pre-emptive multitasking, single-user OS with memory protection. *(Sources: Wikipedia, "Symbian"; Vectree, "Symbian.")*
- **Symbian Ltd.** In June 1998 Psion Software became **Symbian Ltd.**, a joint venture of Psion, Nokia, Ericsson and Motorola (Matsushita joined 1999), and EPOC was renamed **Symbian OS**. Early devices: Ericsson R380 (2000, the first device marketed as a "smartphone"), Nokia 9210 Communicator (2001), Nokia 7650 (2002 — the first S60 phone). From roughly 2003 to 2008 Symbian was the world's leading smartphone OS. *(Sources: Wikipedia, "Symbian"; Vectree, "Symbian.")*
- **Three UI layers over one core.** The core OS was licensed separately from its interfaces: **S60 (Series 60)** — Nokia's, from 2002, designed for one-handed use with a numeric keypad, nav pad and two softkeys; **UIQ** — Sony Ericsson/Motorola, pen-based; **MOAP** — for Japanese NTT DoCoMo handsets. Same kernel family, three different phones on top. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Symbian.")*
- **Series 40** is the other Nokia line and must not be conflated with S60: a proprietary feature-phone platform (first device the Nokia 7110, 1999) with a fixed, manufacturer-controlled UI, no native third-party multitasking OS underneath in the Symbian sense — its third-party story was J2ME (§2.5). Series 40 outsold Symbian for years; it later stretched upward into the Asha line before Nokia's phone business was sold to Microsoft (completed 2014). *(Platform history per Nokia/Wikipedia platform records; the 7110-as-first-S40 point is the commonly documented one.)*
- **Architecture notes a student of the period would insist on:** Symbian's microkernel-ish design (EKA2 kernel from Symbian OS 9), its client–server framework (file server, telephony server ETel), **active objects** as the cooperative-multitasking idiom inside a pre-emptive kernel, descriptors instead of null-terminated strings, and the cleanup stack — a whole C++ dialect shaped by having no exceptions in early versions and tiny RAM budgets. From Symbian OS 9 (2005) came platform security: capabilities and **Symbian Signed**, the signing regime developers loved to hate.
- **The end.** Nokia bought out Symbian Ltd. in 2008 and folded the platform into the Symbian Foundation, which open-sourced the full codebase under the Eclipse Public License in February 2010 — then one of the largest open-source releases ever. The Foundation disintegrated in late 2010; on 11 February 2011 Nokia announced Windows Phone as its primary smartphone platform (the "burning platform" memo), development was outsourced to Accenture, and the **Nokia 808 PureView (2012)** was the last Nokia Symbian smartphone. Final release: Nokia Belle Feature Pack 2, 2 October 2012. Nokia stopped accepting new Symbian apps in January 2014. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Symbian.")*

### 2.2 Palm OS

- **Palm OS 1.0 (1996)** shipped on the PalmPilot: a deliberately minimal OS for a pen-driven PDA — Graffiti handwriting recognition, four core PIM apps (Address, Date Book, Memo Pad, To Do), HotSync desktop synchronisation over a serial cradle. The design philosophy was the opposite of the desktop: instant-on, single-tasking UI, everything a database of records, weeks of battery from two AAA cells.
- **Evolution.** Versions 2.0 (1997) and 3.0 (1998, HotSync matured, web clipping — server-side page shrinking for the Palm VII's wireless service, an early transcoding cousin of Opera Mini's model) followed; 4.0 (2001) added external storage (VFS) and 16-bit colour; **Palm OS 5 "Garnet" (2002)** moved to ARM with the PACE 68k emulator for backward compatibility. **Cobalt (Palm OS 6, 2004)** was a modern multitasking rewrite that famously shipped on no commercial device. Palm's phone line (Treo, running Garnet) carried the platform into the smartphone era until webOS (2009) replaced it entirely. *(Version timeline per Docsity's mobile-OS evolution summary of Palm's release record; Cobalt's no-ship status is the standard documented account.)*

### 2.3 Windows CE → Pocket PC → Windows Mobile

- **Windows CE (1996)** was Microsoft's modular embedded kernel; **Pocket PC 2000** built the PDA shell on CE 3.0, and **Windows Mobile 2003** (CE 4.2) brought Bluetooth and Pocket Internet Explorer to phones and PDAs as one platform. Windows Mobile 5.0 (2005) added persistent storage (no more losing everything when the battery died — a real architectural milestone) and GPS management; 6.0/6.1 (2007–08) and 6.5 (2009) stretched the stylus-era UI over a touch world it wasn't designed for.
- Architecturally it was the desktop pattern miniaturised: Win32-derived APIs, a real file system and registry, pre-emptive multitasking, .NET Compact Framework for managed apps. It treated the phone as a small PC — which is precisely why it won enterprise and lost the consumer. **Windows Phone 7 (October 2010)** broke compatibility completely and replaced it. *(Timeline per Microsoft platform history as summarised in the mobile-OS evolution sources consulted.)*

### 2.4 BlackBerry OS

- **Research In Motion's** platform began with the BlackBerry 850 pager (1999) — two-way email on the Mobitex network — and became a Java-based phone OS whose centre of gravity was never the handset but the **infrastructure**: the BlackBerry Enterprise Server (BES) behind the corporate firewall, the NOC (Network Operations Center) relay, and PIN-to-PIN/BES **push email** with end-to-end encryption for its era. The handset OS itself ran Java applications (and supported MIDP), which is why BlackBerry development felt like J2ME with better email APIs.
- The OS line ran 1999–2013; **BlackBerry 10 (January 2013)**, built on QNX, replaced it. Legacy BlackBerry OS services were finally switched off on 4 January 2022, bricking the classic devices' data services — a clean example of a platform dying at the server, not the handset. *(OS span and BB10 succession per the mobile-platform overviews consulted; the 2022 legacy shutdown per BlackBerry's published end-of-life notices.)*

### 2.5 The runtime layer: J2ME/MIDP and BREW

These two were not operating systems — they were the *portable application layers* that sat on top of whatever OS the manufacturer shipped, and for most of the world's phones they were the entire third-party software story.

- **J2ME / Java ME.** Sun's scaled-down Java: a **configuration** (CLDC — Connected Limited Device Configuration, JSR 30, May 2000; CLDC 1.1, JSR 139, 2002, added floating point) defined the VM and core libraries for devices with 160–512 KB of memory for Java; a **profile** (MIDP — Mobile Information Device Profile, JSR 37, December 2000; MIDP 2.0, JSR 118, November 2002) defined the phone UI (LCDUI), record storage (RMS), and HTTP networking. A MIDlet suite was a JAR + JAD descriptor, typically tens of kilobytes, sandboxed, signed optionally, installed over the air. Java ME shipped on **billions** of devices (Sun/Oracle cited 3+ billion Java ME–enabled phones by 2013) and was the standard app story on Series 40, Sony Ericsson, Motorola and Samsung feature phones, and a supported layer on Symbian and BlackBerry. *(Sources: Wikipedia, "Java Platform, Micro Edition"; JSR dates per the J2ME platform summaries consulted.)*
- **BREW.** Qualcomm's **Binary Runtime Environment for Wireless**, announced 31 January 2001 and first commercially rolled out by Korea's KTF in November 2001: a thin native runtime in C/C++ sitting between applications and the chip system software on Qualcomm's MSM chipsets, with an end-to-end stack — SDK, certification (TRUE BREW testing), the BREW Distribution System for carrier-storefront delivery and billing. Where J2ME was portable-but-sandboxed, BREW was native-speed but carrier-gated and CDMA-centred (Verizon's "Get It Now" was BREW). Opera itself shipped a BREW browser port for KDDI in Japan (announced 25 August 2004) — the two halves of this study touch. *(Sources: Qualcomm press releases, 31 Jan 2001 and 13 Nov 2001; Opera press release, 25 Aug 2004.)*

### 2.6 The network side: GSM, CDMA, and the SIM

**Handset hardware split — the defining architecture question of the era.**

- A **feature phone** runs the GSM protocol stack, the UI and all applications on **one processor — the baseband processor (BP)**: an RTOS-driven chip combining a DSP (Layer 1 signal processing) and an MCU core running Layers 2/3, the UI and the (usually J2ME/BREW-only) apps. One CPU means an application crash can crash the phone. The BP typically also exposed an AT-command serial/USB interface, letting a computer use the phone as a modem. *(Source: Mulliner, Golde & Seifert, "SMS of Death," USENIX Security 2011; Harald Welte, "Anatomy of contemporary GSM cellphone hardware," 2010.)*
- A **smartphone** splits the design: a dedicated **baseband processor** runs the modem stack in isolation, and a separate **application processor (AP)** runs the rich OS and apps, the two talking over a serial link with AT commands (later RIL-style interfaces). The first smartphones were literally a feature phone and a PDA in one case, with the phone half's keypad and screen removed. The modem itself decomposes into RF front-end (the Um air interface), analog baseband (modulation/demodulation, I/Q), and digital baseband (DSP + protocol stack L1–L3). *(Source: Welte, "Anatomy of contemporary GSM cellphone hardware," 2010.)*
- **SoC integration** then spent a decade collapsing the boxes: TI's OMAP (AP + DSP), Qualcomm's MSM series (BP + BREW + eventually the AP — the direct ancestor of Snapdragon), MediaTek's turnkey feature-phone SoCs (which put a whole phone — baseband, RTOS, UI reference design — on one cheap chip and enabled the ultra-low-cost handset flood of the late 2000s). The single-chip phone is why a working phone could cost what it costs today.

**GSM network architecture (2G).**

- Radio side: handset (MS) → **BTS** (base transceiver station) → **BSC** (base station controller). Core: the **MSC** switches calls, with a **VLR** holding visiting subscribers' temporary records; the **HLR** is the master subscriber database; the **AuC** holds authentication keys; the **EIR** tracks equipment identities (IMEI); the **SMSC** store-and-forwards text messages. Signalling ran over SS7 with the Mobile Application Part (MAP). **GPRS** (2.5G) bolted packet data on with two new nodes — **SGSN** (mobility/session) and **GGSN** (gateway to the internet) — and EDGE (2.75G) raised radio rates by changing the modulation. *(Sources: TU Berlin telecommunications lecture materials; Welte 2010.)*
- **CDMA (the other 2G).** Qualcomm's IS-95/cdmaOne and its successor cdma2000 took the opposite radio philosophy: every call shares the whole channel, separated by spreading codes, with fast closed-loop power control and soft handoff between cells. Its network elements have GSM analogues (BTS/BSC/MSC/HLR), and its application layer was BREW rather than J2ME — the GSM/CDMA split ran all the way up the stack, from physics to app store.

**The SIM — a computer in the phone that isn't the phone's.**

- The **SIM** (Subscriber Identity Module) is a smart card with its own CPU, ROM, RAM and EEPROM file system (standardised in GSM 11.11): it holds the **IMSI** (subscriber identity) and the secret key **Ki**, which exists in exactly two places — the SIM and the network's AuC. Authentication is challenge–response: the network sends RAND, the SIM computes SRES and the cipher key Kc using A3/A8 (COMP128 in most deployments), and Ki itself never leaves the card. *(Source: TU Berlin lecture materials — "Only HLR [AuC] and SIM know Ki"; GSM 11.11.)*
- **SIM Application Toolkit (STK, GSM 11.14)** let the card itself run tiny applications — proactive commands that put menus on the phone, send SMS, or set up calls — which is how carriers shipped banking and info services on phones with no app platform at all. The SIM was, in effect, the first always-present third-party runtime in mobile, owned by the carrier. Later USIMs on Java Card extended the same idea.

### 2.7 The first mobile webs: WAP, i-mode, and the microbrowsers

- **WAP (Wireless Application Protocol).** Announced 26 June 1997 by Ericsson, Motorola, Nokia and Unwired Planet (later Openwave); WAP 1.0 followed in April 1998; the first commercial service launched with Telfort in the Netherlands in October 1999 (the Nokia 7110 was its emblematic handset). WAP rebuilt the whole web stack for 9.6 kbps circuit-switched data: **WML** (an XML language of *decks* and *cards* — fetch a deck once, navigate cards locally to save round trips), **WMLScript**, 1-bit **WBMP** images, and a parallel protocol stack (WSP/WTP/WTLS/WDP) replacing HTTP/TCP/TLS/UDP. Between phone and internet sat the **WAP gateway** in the carrier's machine room: the phone spoke binary-encoded WAP to the gateway; the gateway fetched ordinary HTTP, transcoded, and pushed content down. Two structural lessons: (1) the gateway made the carrier the mandatory middleman of every request; (2) WAP 1.x had the famous *WAP gap* — WTLS from phone to gateway, TLS from gateway to server, plaintext inside the gateway, so no true end-to-end encryption. **WAP 2.0** (public review 1 August 2001) conceded the point: XHTML Mobile Profile instead of WML, real TCP/IP and HTTP/1.1, real TLS. The WAP Forum folded into the Open Mobile Alliance in 2002. *(Source: "Dead End: WAP and the Mobile Web," geschichte-der-informatik.de; WAP Forum white-paper summaries.)*
- **i-mode.** NTT DoCoMo launched i-mode on 22 February 1999 (team under Keiichi Enoki; service design by Mari Matsunaga; business model by Takeshi Natsuno). Instead of a parallel stack, i-mode used **cHTML** — a subset of ordinary HTML — over a packet-switched network (always-on, billed per packet, not per minute), with an official-site portal and a revenue split that paid content providers the large majority of subscription fees collected on the phone bill. It reached ~30 million subscribers by 2001 while WAP was still being explained. The contrast is the era's cleanest experiment: WAP optimised the *protocol* and made the carrier the gatekeeper; i-mode optimised the *business model* and the authoring cost (any web author already knew cHTML) — and i-mode won its home market outright. *(Sources: Stanford GSCMF i-mode case study, 2001; "Dead End: WAP and the Mobile Web.")*
- **The microbrowsers.** Before and beside Opera: **Openwave's UP.Browser** (from Unwired Planet/Phone.com, HDML then WML — the browser inside most WAP phones); **ACCESS NetFront** (the embedded HTML browser family from Japan's ACCESS, standard on i-mode handsets and later on game consoles and the PSP/PS3); Handspring's **Blazer** on Palm OS; and various vendor WAP browsers. Each was a study in the same constraints: kilobytes of RAM, monochrome or 128-pixel screens, per-minute or per-kilobyte billing. **[GAP]** — this pass did not pull primary sources for NetFront's version-by-version history; its role as the i-mode-era embedded browser is the documented headline, and finer detail is left unclaimed.
- **The capstone: Opera Mini as the gateway idea, perfected.** Opera Mini (2005) is what WAP wanted to be: instead of demanding the world rewrite itself in WML, Opera's servers fetched the *real* web and transcoded it down to the phone (OBML instead of binary WML; Opera's commercial proxy fleet instead of the carrier's gateway; end-to-end encryption from phone to Opera's servers instead of the WAP gap). The market verdict is in Part 1: 168.8 million active users by March 2012. *(Source: Wikipedia, "Opera Mobile.")*

## Part 3 — Assimilation Notes: What This Knowledge Does in Douglas's Current Work

1. **Cheap phones are the feature-phone pattern repeating.** A $50–$100 Android phone is the MediaTek turnkey phone of 2026: one integrated SoC, modest RAM, metered or unreliable data. The design rules that era proved still apply verbatim — assume the network is slow and priced, assume memory is scarce, make the first screen useful in one round trip (the WML deck lesson), and never let an app crash take the whole experience down (the single-CPU lesson, now: keep WebView content from being able to wedge the shell).
2. **WebView APKs are the new MIDlet.** His WebView-based APKs occupy exactly the niche J2ME/BREW filled: a small, installable, sandboxed client whose heavy lifting happens elsewhere. The MIDlet discipline transfers directly — tiny package, declarative descriptor (then JAD, now the manifest), graceful behaviour at 160 KB thinking even when the budget is larger. A ~13 KB debug APK would have been a *large* MIDlet; the era's instincts about size are a feature, not nostalgia.
3. **Proxy/compression thinking is token thinking.** Opera Mini's whole thesis — *move the work to the cheap side of the expensive link, and send back only the rendered result* — is the same shape as his cheapest-lane doctrine for AI work: the expensive link is now tokens and metered API time instead of per-kilobyte GPRS, and the "transcoding server" is the free/local lane (local scripts, Gemini's lane, cached files) that fetches, digests and compresses before anything crosses the metered link. OBML's lesson is precise: a pre-laid-out, already-digested snapshot is cheaper to receive than the raw material plus the work of rendering it — summaries and prepared packets first, raw archives only when needed.
4. **The gateway lesson, kept honest.** WAP failed where its gateway was a toll-booth and a plaintext gap; Opera Mini succeeded where its proxy was a servant and encrypted. Applied here: any middleman layer in his stack (sync scripts, mirrors, relays) must be auditable, encrypted where it carries anything private, and removable — a gateway you cannot inspect or bypass eventually owns you.
5. **Old-with-new is the never-lose ledger, generalised.** Opera's own history shows the cost of the opposite rule: when Link closed, Presto users' sync simply ended; when My Opera closed, four months' warning was all that stood between a community and deletion. His standing rule — retired things are *recorded*, formats are kept readable, old versions sit beside new — is the preservation policy this history argues for. The ledger in §1.5 is the template: every retired feature in his own stack (scripts, APK versions, skills) gets a line with dates and its replacement, and nothing is deleted for being old.
6. **The SIM principle: keep one small thing that is truly yours.** The most durable component of the entire 2G architecture was a removable card holding one secret that never left it, portable across every handset. The modern analogue in his work: a small, portable, self-contained core — his files, his record, his keys — that survives any single device or platform being replaced, exactly as a SIM survived every phone it was ever put into.

## Simply put

Twenty years ago, phones were too weak and data was too expensive for the real internet, so clever people built shortcuts. Opera Mini was the best shortcut: big computers in Norway squeezed web pages down to a tenth of their size and sent the squeezed version to tiny phones. WAP tried the same trick but made everyone rewrite their websites, so it mostly failed; Japan's i-mode kept things simple and made sure the people making content got paid, so it thrived. The phones themselves ran systems like Symbian, Palm and Windows Mobile, plus little Java programs, on chips that split the "radio" work from the "thinking" work — and a tiny card, the SIM, held your identity through all of it. Almost all of those systems are retired now, but retired doesn't mean worthless: the tricks they invented — squeeze the data, do the heavy work somewhere cheap, keep the small important thing safe and portable — are the same tricks that make cheap phones and small apps work well today.

## His perspective (Douglas's, from his dictation)
The other issue is party line wiring. Party lines in the old days forced more than one house onto the same set of two wires, with more than one ring pattern on one pair. A second set of wires often ran beside the first — for the emergency system, and in a lot of towns that second pair carried data, and the rich people — doctors, lawyers — had those private lines. Some of those places may have been accidentally dialed in or out because of teletypes, fax machines, and the other early technologies that might still be active in certain places. Old plant doesn't retire itself; it stays in the ground and in the walls until somebody digs it up and replaces it, and everything new still has to talk to it.

## Ember's perspective (the technical record, from her own knowledge)
**The pair.** Classic telephone service is one copper pair per line — tip and ring. Everything below is different ways of sharing or signaling on pairs like it.

**Party lines.** On a party line, two to ten households shared ONE pair. The central office rang all the phones; *whose* ring it was came from the ring pattern — coded ringing (long-short, two shorts, and so on) or, on better plant, selective ringing by frequency or by which side of the pair was rung. Privacy was a social contract, not a technology: anyone on the pair could lift a receiver and hear. His memory of "more than one ring on the same set of two wires" is exactly coded ringing.

**The second pair.** A household or office that needed certainty paid for a private line — its own pair, one ring, no neighbors. Doctors and lawyers were the classic private-line customers precisely because their calls couldn't be shared or missed. In many buildings and towns, additional pairs in the same cable carried alarm circuits, teletype loops, and later fax and data — which is why old cable bundles are a layered archaeology: voice pairs, private pairs, alarm pairs, data pairs, side by side in one sheath.

**Pulse dialing.** A rotary dial signals by *interrupting* the loop current: dial a 7 and the dial's contacts break the circuit seven times, at about ten pulses per second, and the switch counts the breaks. It works on any pair, needs no power-hungry electronics in the phone, and the receiving gear is simple counting machinery — which is why pulse refuses to die: elevator phones, alarm panels, and old PBXs may still speak it.

**Tone dialing (DTMF).** Touch-Tone replaced counting with music: each key presses two simultaneous tones from a grid (rows 697–941 Hz, columns 1209–1633 Hz), and the switch listens for the pair. Faster, and — the part that changed the world — the tones travel *in the voice band itself*, so they keep working after the call connects: voicemail menus, phone trees, and "press 1" are all DTMF talking down a live call. Pulse could never do that; once connected, its clicks meant nothing downstream.

**Why this still bites.** Teletypes (and their descendants, TTY/TDD devices for deaf users), fax machines, postage meters, alarm panels, and modem-era gear all lived on these pairs and some still do. A fax machine auto-answering on a shared or mis-wired pair, an alarm panel seizing a line, a cross-connected pair in a fifty-year-old splice case — any of them can make a call land in the wrong place or not land at all, and none of it will ever appear in the phone company's switch software, because the fault is physical, in the plant, exactly as Douglas keeps saying: the fix, where the pair is wrong, is a person, in person, at the wire.

## The one-line version for the packet
Pulse counts breaks, tone sings pairs, party lines shared one pair among neighbors with coded rings, private pairs cost money and carried the doctors — and all of it is still in the ground underneath the internet, which still rides it.

## 1. Executive summary

A Wisconsin resident does not live under one law. He lives under a *stack* of laws at the same time, made by different lawmakers, enforced by different officers, and heard in different courts:

1. **The U.S. Constitution and federal law** (Congress, federal agencies, federal courts) — the top layer. Under the Supremacy Clause (U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2), a valid federal law wins any direct conflict with state law [the record — Cornell LII, Constitution Annotated].
2. **The Wisconsin Constitution and the Wisconsin Statutes** (the Legislature in Madison, state agencies, Wisconsin courts) — the layer that governs most of daily life: crimes, driving, renting, working, buying, voting in state elections, state taxes, and state-run benefits.
3. **Administrative rules** — the detailed regulations written by agencies under authority the legislatures gave them (federal rules in the Code of Federal Regulations; Wisconsin rules in the Wisconsin Administrative Code, e.g., ATCP 134 on rental practices). Rules have the force of law inside their lane, but an agency cannot outrun its statute.
4. **Local ordinances** — county boards, city councils, village and town boards (for Douglas's area: Chippewa Falls and Eau Claire County; the Willard area in Clark County). Zoning, noise, parking, local permits, some traffic and public-health matters live here.
5. **Tribal law** — a *separate sovereign layer*, not a rung on the ladder above. Federally recognized tribes are "domestic dependent nations" with inherent self-government authority [the record — Cherokee Nation v. Georgia, 30 U.S. 1 (1831); Cornell LII, American Indian Law]. Wisconsin adds a specific wrinkle: Public Law 280 (18 U.S.C. § 1162; 28 U.S.C. § 1360) gives the *state* criminal and limited civil jurisdiction in most Indian country in Wisconsin, with the Menominee Reservation treated differently after retrocession [the record — Public Law 280 text, govinfo.gov; Wisconsin Attorney General opinions].

**The one-sentence mechanics:** when two layers genuinely conflict, federal law usually wins (preemption) — *unless* the federal law was written as a floor rather than a ceiling, in which case a stricter state law stands beside it and both must be obeyed (HIPAA privacy is the textbook example: 45 C.F.R. § 160.203(b) preserves "more stringent" state law) [the record].

**What this means for Ember's help [his framing, operationalized in §6]:** research through public and legal channels only; stay inside platform terms; treat Wisconsin's one-party-consent recording statute (Wis. Stat. § 968.31) as the operative rule when recording in Wisconsin; never facilitate access that a law or a system's terms reserve to someone else.

### Simply put

Think of law like weather systems stacked over one house in Wisconsin. The biggest system is the country's — the U.S. Constitution and federal law, made in Washington. Under it is Wisconsin's own system, made in Madison, and that is the one you feel most days: the road rules, the renting rules, the rules for work, shopping, voting, and state benefits. Under that are the rulebooks the agencies write to fill in details, and the small local rules from your county and town. Tribal nations have their *own* weather — they are separate governments, not just another layer of Wisconsin. When two rules truly clash, the federal rule usually wins — but sometimes Congress only set a *minimum*, and then Wisconsin is allowed to be stricter, and you follow the stricter one. Douglas's instruction to Ember is simple: know which rules he has chosen to live by, and keep all of our work inside them.

## 2. The layered structure

### 2.1 Layer one — the U.S. Constitution and federal law

The Constitution is the highest law in the country; every statute, rule, ordinance, and court decision beneath it must fit inside it. Below the Constitution sit:

- **Federal statutes** — laws passed by Congress and signed by the President, collected in the United States Code (e.g., the Wiretap Act at 18 U.S.C. §§ 2510–2523; the Food and Nutrition Act framework for SNAP at 7 U.S.C. § 2020) [the record].
- **Treaties** — named in the Supremacy Clause itself as supreme law alongside the Constitution and federal statutes [the record — art. VI, cl. 2 text].
- **Federal regulations** — agency rules in the Code of Federal Regulations (C.F.R.), valid only within the authority Congress delegated.
- **Federal court decisions** — the U.S. Supreme Court's reading of the Constitution binds every court in the country; the federal district for most of northwestern Wisconsin is the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Wisconsin, with appeals to the Seventh Circuit [Ember's note: district/circuit assignment stated from general structure; court assignment for a specific filing should be confirmed before reliance].

Federal power is broad but not unlimited: Congress acts through enumerated powers (taxing, spending, interstate commerce, and others), and the Tenth Amendment reserves the powers not delegated to the states or the people [the record — Cornell LII discussion of the Tenth Amendment]. Ordinary "police power" — health, safety, and everyday criminal law — belongs mainly to the states.

### 2.2 Layer two — the Wisconsin Constitution and Wisconsin Statutes

Wisconsin has its own constitution (1848), its own legislature, governor, and court system (circuit courts, Court of Appeals, Wisconsin Supreme Court). The Wisconsin Statutes are organized by chapter, and the chapters are the working map of a resident's daily legal life:

- **Criminal law** — the criminal code and criminal procedure occupy the 900-series chapters (notably chs. 939–951 and ch. 968 on arrests, warrants, and electronic surveillance) [Ember's note: chapter-range statement is structural; § 968.31 itself was verified directly, see §4.10].
- **Traffic** — ch. 346 is the rules-of-the-road chapter named in the commission for this study [chapter title not re-verified line-by-line in this pass — treat the chapter number as the Legislature's own numbering and confirm at docs.legis.wisconsin.gov before quoting a specific section].
- **Consumer protection** — ch. 100 (trade practices), enforced chiefly by the Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection (DATCP); § 100.20 is the unfair-practices engine that powers rules like ATCP 134 [the record — DATCP Landlord Tenant Guide; Wisconsin cases applying § 100.20(5)].
- **Landlord–tenant** — ch. 704 plus ATCP 134 [the record, see §5.4].
- **Employment** — Wisconsin's fair-employment and wage law sits principally in ch. 111 and related chapters [chapter attribution not individually re-verified in this pass].
- **Elections** — principally chs. 5–12 [chapter-range attribution not individually re-verified in this pass].
- **State taxes** — principally ch. 71 (income/franchise tax) [chapter attribution not individually re-verified in this pass], administered by the Department of Revenue.
- **Public benefits** — ch. 49 is the public-assistance chapter; FoodShare (SNAP) administration runs under Wis. Stat. §§ 49.79 and 49.797 [the record — Wisconsin DHS filing citing those sections and 7 U.S.C. § 2020].

### 2.3 Layer three — administrative rules

Legislatures cannot write every detail, so they delegate. Two rulebooks matter to a Wisconsin resident:

- **Federal:** the C.F.R. Example: HIPAA's privacy rules live at 45 C.F.R. Parts 160 and 164, under the HIPAA statute itself at 42 U.S.C. § 1320d et seq. [the record — 45 C.F.R. § 160.203 preemption rule and § 1320d citation verified].
- **Wisconsin:** the Wisconsin Administrative Code. Example: **ATCP 134, "Residential Rental Practices,"** adopted under Wis. Stat. § 100.20 and administered by DATCP, which is why a landlord who violates ATCP 134 can face the § 100.20 remedies, including a tenant's suit for double pecuniary loss under § 100.20(5) [the record — DATCP guide; Wisconsin Court of Appeals discussion in State v. Lasecki, 2020 WI App 36].

Since *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), federal courts no longer defer to an agency's reading of an ambiguous statute the way they did under *Chevron*; courts exercise independent judgment about what a statute means [the record — noted in Cornell/legal summaries of the Supremacy Clause materials reviewed]. The practical point for a resident: an agency rule is real law, but it can be challenged as exceeding its statute.

### 2.4 Layer four — local ordinances

Counties, cities, villages, and towns legislate locally under authority the state grants them: zoning and land use, noise and nuisance, parking and some local road rules, building and health codes, licensing of local businesses and animals. For Douglas's geography [from his records on file]: **Chippewa Falls** (city, Chippewa County), **Eau Claire County**, the **Augusta/Fairchild** area (Eau Claire/Jackson County side), and **Willard** (town in **Clark County**). The same conduct can be lawful under state law and still violate a local ordinance — ordinances are the layer most residents meet through a ticket or a permit denial rather than a courtroom.

### 2.5 Layer five — tribal law, a separate sovereign

Tribal law does **not** sit underneath Wisconsin law. Federally recognized tribes are political sovereigns whose authority to govern themselves predates the United States; federal law describes them as "domestic dependent nations" [the record — Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831)]. The working principles:

- Tribes retain every attribute of inherent sovereign authority that Congress has not taken away or that has not been necessarily divested [the record — Cornell LII, American Indian Law; U.S. v. Lara, 541 U.S. 193 (2004), on inherent criminal jurisdiction over Indians].
- State law generally has **no force** in Indian country on its own — Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. 515 (1832): state laws "can have no force" on Cherokee land [the record].
- Congress holds plenary power over Indian affairs (Indian Commerce Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8), and the federal government carries a trust responsibility toward tribes [the record].
- Tribal courts adjudicate matters within tribal jurisdiction; the Indian Civil Rights Act (25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303) applies a Bill-of-Rights-like set of limits to tribal governments [Ember's note: ICRA citation is standard; § 1301(2) was verified in the Lara materials reviewed].
- **Wisconsin-specific:** Congress, in **Public Law 280 (1953)**, granted Wisconsin (and a few other states) state criminal jurisdiction over offenses by or against Indians in Indian country, and limited state civil *adjudicatory* jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1360) — originally excepting the Menominee Reservation; Wisconsin later **retroceded** Menominee jurisdiction to the United States (effective March 1, 1976, under 25 U.S.C. § 1323), so the map is not uniform even inside one state [the record — PL 280 text at govinfo.gov; Latender v. Israel, 584 F.2d 817 (7th Cir.), recounting the retrocession]. PL 280 did **not** give the state general civil regulatory power (taxing, zoning) over tribes, and it preserved treaty hunting and fishing rights [the record — § 1162(b) protections for trust property and water rights in the statute text reviewed].

[Ember's note] This section states general principles only, per the commission. Which sovereign's law governs a specific event on specific land is one of the most fact-sensitive questions in American law; nothing here adjudicates any matter of Douglas's, and his own accounts of tribal-law matters are his accounts, filed as his elsewhere, not examined here.

### 2.6 When layers conflict — supremacy and preemption, plainly

**The rule** [the record — U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2; Cornell LII, Constitution Annotated]:

> "This Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof; and all Treaties made … under the Authority of the United States, shall be the supreme Law of the Land; and the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby, any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding."

**How courts apply it — preemption, in four shapes** [the record — Cornell LII, Modern Doctrine on Supremacy Clause]:

1. **Express preemption** — the federal statute says in so many words that it displaces state law on the subject.
2. **Field preemption** — federal regulation of a subject is so pervasive that courts infer Congress left no room for states to add to it (immigration is the modern example, Arizona v. United States, 567 U.S. 387 (2012)).
3. **Conflict preemption** — obeying both is impossible, or the state law stands as an obstacle to what Congress was trying to do.
4. **No preemption at all** — the most common outcome in daily life. Courts start with a *presumption against preemption* in fields states have traditionally handled (health, safety, crime), and most federal statutes regulate alongside state law rather than instead of it.

**The exceptions a resident actually meets:**

- **Floor vs. ceiling.** Some federal laws set a *floor*: states may be stricter but not weaker. HIPAA is the model — a contrary state law is generally preempted, **except** a state law that is "more stringent" in protecting health-information privacy, which survives and controls (45 C.F.R. §§ 160.202, 160.203(b)) [the record]. Wages, environmental rules, and much consumer law work the same way.
- **Federal law only reaches what Congress may reach.** A federal statute outside Congress's constitutional powers claims no supremacy at all ("made in Pursuance thereof").
- **Anti-commandeering.** The federal government may regulate people directly, but it may not order a state legislature to legislate or conscript state officers to administer a federal program (New York v. United States (1992); Printz v. United States (1997)) [the record — Cornell summaries reviewed].
- **Spending power as the bridge.** Where Congress cannot command, it often pays: federal money with federal conditions attached, administered by the state. That is the constitutional shape of Medicaid and SNAP/FoodShare (§§3–4).

### Simply put

The Constitution is the roof. Federal law is the strongest beam — when it truly bumps into a state rule, the state rule gives way. But Washington never claimed the whole house. Madison built most of the rooms you actually live in, the agencies wrote the instruction sheets taped inside the cupboards, your county and town set the house rules by the door, and the tribal nations own their own houses next door — related to yours by treaties and by Congress, not by being your basement. And sometimes the federal beam is only a *minimum height*: Wisconsin is allowed to build the wall higher, and then you follow the higher wall.

## 3. How federal law reaches a Wisconsin resident

Federal law touches a Wisconsin resident less often day-to-day than state law — but when it does, it usually arrives with the full weight of the national government behind it.

### 3.1 Civil rights and constitutional rights

The U.S. Constitution's Bill of Rights, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, is the resident's shield against *government* action at every level — a city ordinance, a state statute, and a federal practice are all measured against the same First, Fourth, Fifth, and Fourteenth Amendments. Federal civil-rights statutes (e.g., the Civil Rights Act of 1964; 42 U.S.C. § 1983 suits for violations of constitutional rights under color of state law) give a resident claims that exist *because* federal law says so, whatever state law says [Ember's note: standard federal framework; specific statutory citations in this paragraph are structural and were not individually re-verified in this pass].

### 3.2 Federal courts

A resident meets federal court in three main ways: a case arising under federal law (federal question), a suit against or by the United States, or a large civil dispute between citizens of different states (diversity). Federal criminal charges (federal crimes, federal land, interstate conduct) are prosecuted by the U.S. Attorney in federal court — separate sovereign, separate case, even for the same underlying act (the dual-sovereignty doctrine) [the record — doctrine illustrated in U.S. v. Lara for the tribal/federal pair].

### 3.3 Taxes — the IRS layer

The federal income tax (Internal Revenue Code, Title 26) applies to a Wisconsin resident directly: filing, withholding, refunds, and audit authority are federal, run by the Internal Revenue Service. Wisconsin then layers its own income tax on top (§5.5), starting from federal figures in places but under its own statute, rates, and Department of Revenue. A resident answers to both, every year, independently [Ember's note: Title 26 / IRS structure is general knowledge of the federal system; no specific I.R.C. section is relied on here].

### 3.4 Benefits — federal rules, federal money, state counters

- **Social Security (SSA)** — retirement, disability (SSDI), and survivors' benefits are purely federal: federal eligibility rules, federal money, federal agency, federal appeals path, wherever the resident lives.
- **Medicare** — federal health coverage for 65+ and certain disabled persons: federal rules, federal payment, administered nationally (through CMS and its contractors). Where you live changes your plan options, not the program's legal nature.
- **Medicaid / BadgerCare Plus** — the classic joint program: Congress sets the statutory frame and pays a large share; Wisconsin writes a state plan, sets covered services within federal limits, and runs enrollment through DHS and county agencies. The Wisconsin Department of Health Services describes Medicaid as "a joint federal and state program," the single largest program in the state budget, covering over a million Wisconsinites [the record — DHS 2023-25 Biennial Report].
- **SNAP / FoodShare** — federal nutrition law and federal benefit dollars — USDA funds 100% of benefits; Wisconsin DHS administers the program day-to-day under Wis. Stat. §§ 49.79 and 49.797 and 7 U.S.C. § 2020(a), with administrative costs split under 7 U.S.C. § 2025(a); eligibility is determined through the state's CARES system and county/tribal agencies, and benefits land on the Wisconsin QUEST card [the record — Wisconsin DHS declaration and DHS FoodShare publications reviewed].

### 3.5 HIPAA and health privacy

HIPAA (statute at 42 U.S.C. § 1320d et seq.; Privacy and Security Rules at 45 C.F.R. Parts 160, 164) governs how health plans, providers, and their business associates use and disclose protected health information [the record]. Three resident-level facts:

1. HIPAA binds *covered entities* — clinics, hospitals, insurers — more than it binds the patient; it gives the patient rights (access to records, an accounting of disclosures, the right to ask for restrictions) rather than a general personal lawsuit.
2. HIPAA is a **floor, not a ceiling** — see §5.2 for how Wisconsin's own records law stacks on top of it.
3. HIPAA does not erase stricter federal pockets: substance-use-disorder treatment records under 42 C.F.R. Part 2 have historically carried tighter consent rules [the record — referenced in the Wisconsin DHS/HIPAA materials reviewed].

### 3.6 Telecommunications and the FCC

Broadcasting, interstate phone and internet service, spectrum, and robocall rules are federally led (Communications Act of 1934; the FCC), with states keeping a role in intrastate service and consumer complaints through bodies like the Public Service Commission of Wisconsin. The federal recording statute lives here too (see §4.10): the federal Wiretap Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2511, is enacted under this federal umbrella [the record — § 2511(2)(d) text verified via Cornell LII].

### 3.7 Interstate matters and federal enclaves

- **Interstate commerce and travel:** federal law governs the channels — interstate trucking and aviation safety, mail, and crimes that cross state lines. A Wisconsin resident driving to Minnesota keeps his Wisconsin license by interstate compact/reciprocity, while answering to Minnesota's road rules while there (§6.4).
- **Federal lands and agencies:** national forests (Chequamegon–Nicolet in northern Wisconsin), federal buildings, military installations (e.g., Fort McCoy, near Sparta in Monroe County), and federal enclaves carry federal rules and, under the Assimilative Crimes Act (18 U.S.C. § 13), often borrow the surrounding state's criminal law for acts not covered by federal statute [Ember's note: § 13 citation is the standard mechanism; not individually re-verified in this pass]. Access to such installations is by the installation's own rules and permission — a point that maps directly to Douglas's own Nov 9, 2026 settlement [his framing]: Fort McCoy material is reached through public areas and lawful public channels only.
- **Federal officers and benefits offices** — SSA field offices, VA facilities, and post offices bring federal employment, safety, and conduct rules with them into a Wisconsin town.

## 4. How Wisconsin state law reaches a resident

State law is the water a Wisconsin resident swims in daily. The main channels, in the order the commission named them:

### 4.1 Criminal code, basics

Wisconsin defines crimes and penalties in its statutes, classifies felonies and misdemeanors by class, and runs prosecutions through county district attorneys in circuit court. Police power — the authority to criminalize conduct for health, safety, and welfare — is the state's home turf; the presumption against preemption in §2.6 runs strongest here. Procedure matters as much as substance: arrest, search, and surveillance rules come from both the U.S. Constitution (Fourth Amendment) and the Wisconsin Constitution and statutes, and evidence gathered outside them can be excluded [Ember's note: structural summary; the one surveillance statute examined in depth in this study is § 968.31, §4.10].

### 4.2 Traffic — ch. 346 and its neighbors

Driving is a state-law world almost end to end: licensing (Department of Transportation, Division of Motor Vehicles), vehicle registration and equipment, rules of the road (ch. 346, per the commission's citation), OWI law, and point/driver-record systems are Wisconsin's. Federal law enters mainly at the edges — commercial driver's licenses under federal standards, federal highway money with conditions (e.g., the 0.08 BAC and 21-year drinking-age funding conditions historically), and federal motor-carrier safety rules for interstate trucking [Ember's note: chapter number as commissioned; individual traffic sections were not re-verified in this pass].

### 4.3 Consumer protection — DATCP and ch. 100

Chapter 100 is Wisconsin's trade-practices chapter. Section 100.20 prohibits unfair methods of competition and unfair trade practices and authorizes DATCP to write rules (the ATCP chapters) and to enforce them; § 100.20(5) gives an injured person a private action for pecuniary loss — doubled, with costs and attorney fees, when the violation is of a rule issued under § 100.20, as the landlord-tenant cases show [the record — Lasecki; DATCP materials]. DATCP is also the resident's complaint counter for everything from rental practices to home-improvement and telemarketing disputes.

### 4.4 Landlord–tenant — ch. 704 and ATCP 134

[The record — DATCP Landlord Tenant Guide; Wis. Attorney General Op. OAG-04-13; Lasecki.]

- **Ch. 704** sets the statute-level frame: rental agreements, landlord and tenant duties (§ 704.07 — the landlord's duty to keep premises in a reasonable state of repair cannot be waived by the lease, § 704.07(1)), security-deposit limits and the 21-day return/withholding rule (§ 704.28), and prohibited lease provisions that void an agreement (§ 704.44).
- **ATCP 134** fills in the business practices: earnest money and pre-tenancy disclosures (§ 134.03–.05), the written statement accounting for anything withheld from a deposit (§ 134.06(4)(a)), and prohibited practices (§ 134.08, e.g., waiving the landlord's legal obligations).
- **Enforcement is real on both ends:** DATCP investigates complaints; a tenant's § 100.20(5) suit can recover double losses; and an intentional violation of a § 100.20 rule can carry criminal exposure under § 100.26(3) — a combination the Court of Appeals walked through step by step in *Lasecki*, affirming misdemeanor convictions of a landlord who failed to account for withheld deposits [the record].

### 4.5 Employment

State law sets the floor under the job beside federal law: Wisconsin's fair-employment law (discrimination in hiring and firing), wage payment and hours rules, unemployment insurance (a federal-state program: federal tax and frame, state administration and benefits), and worker's compensation — a state system that largely replaces lawsuits for workplace injury [Ember's note: program-structure summary; specific chapter citations (principally ch. 111 for fair employment; ch. 102 for worker's compensation; ch. 108 for unemployment) were not individually re-verified in this pass].

### 4.6 Elections

Federal elections are run *by the states* under the Constitution's Elections Clause (art. I, § 4): Wisconsin law (principally chs. 5–12) sets registration, ID, absentee, and polling rules for federal and state races alike, administered by the Wisconsin Elections Commission and municipal clerks — subject to federal floors like the Voting Rights Act, the National Voter Registration Act, and the Help America Vote Act [Ember's note: framework summary; chapter range as commissioned, not individually re-verified].

### 4.7 State taxes

Wisconsin taxes in parallel with the IRS: an individual income tax collected by the Department of Revenue, a state sales/use tax (5%) with county add-ons in many counties, property tax administered locally (the main funding for schools, towns, and counties), and excise taxes (fuel, tobacco) shared between levels [Ember's note: the 5% state sales-tax rate is widely published state information; it was not re-verified against a primary source in this pass and should be confirmed at revenue.wisconsin.gov before any filing decision].

### 4.8 Public benefits at the state counter

§3.4 described the programs' federal skeletons; the resident meets them at state counters: ACCESS (access.wi.gov) for FoodShare, BadgerCare Plus, and related applications; county human-services agencies and tribal agencies as the local offices; the QUEST card in the grocery line. Long-term-care programs (Family Care/IRIS) and state supplements to federal SSI follow the same joint pattern [the record for FoodShare/Medicaid structure — DHS materials; Ember's note: Family Care/IRIS named as examples of the pattern, not separately examined].

### 4.9 Health-care consent and records — chs. 51 and 146

[The record — Wis. Stat. §§ 146.81–146.82, 146.816, 146.819, 51.30, as published by the Wisconsin Legislature.]

- **Wis. Stat. § 146.82** is Wisconsin's patient-records confidentiality statute: *all* patient health care records are confidential and may be released only as the section allows or with the patient's informed consent (§ 146.82(1)), followed by a long, specific list of releases permitted without consent (§ 146.82(2)).
- **§ 146.81(4)** defines "patient health care records" broadly — but expressly *carves out* records governed by **§ 51.30**, the separate, tighter regime for mental-health, developmental-disability, and substance-use treatment records [the record — definition text reviewed].
- **§ 146.816** coordinates the two worlds: a covered entity's use or disclosure that complies with the HIPAA rules (45 C.F.R. §§ 164.500–164.534) is, in the circumstances the section specifies, not blocked by §§ 51.30(4)(a) and (e) or 146.82 — the Legislature's own bridge between the state and federal privacy systems [the record].
- **Consent law generally:** who may consent to treatment, informed-consent duties of providers, and surrogate decision-making are state-law subjects (statute and common law), which is why hospital consent forms look Wisconsin-shaped even inside a federally funded system.

### 4.10 The recording statute — Wis. Stat. § 968.31, stated accurately and neutrally

[The record — full statutory text at docs.legis.wisconsin.gov.]

Wisconsin's electronic-surveillance statute, **§ 968.31**, makes it a **Class H felony** to intentionally intercept a wire, electronic, or oral communication, intentionally use a device to intercept an oral communication, or disclose/use unlawfully intercepted contents (§ 968.31(1)), and then lists conduct that is **not** unlawful (§ 968.31(2)). The provision this study was asked to state — the **one-party-consent exception**, § 968.31(2)(c) — provides, in substance and close to its words:

> It is not unlawful for a person *not* acting under color of law to intercept a wire, electronic, or oral communication **where the person is a party to the communication, or where one of the parties has given prior consent** to the interception — **unless** the interception is done for the purpose of committing a criminal or tortious act, or any other injurious act.

Four neutral qualifications that are part of the statute itself, not commentary:

1. **The federal statute is parallel.** The federal Wiretap Act's one-party exception, 18 U.S.C. § 2511(2)(d), is worded nearly identically, including the same criminal/tortious-purpose carve-back [the record — § 2511(2)(d) via Cornell LII].
2. **"One-party consent" is a summary, not the whole law.** The statute also contains separate exceptions (provider employees in the ordinary course, § 968.31(2)(a); law enforcement under color of law, (2)(b); publicly accessible communications, (2)(d)–(e)), and separate offenses for disclosure and use. Court annotations recognize consent may in some settings be implied from circumstances (State v. Riley, 2005 WI App 203) [the record — annotations in the statute as published].
3. **Other laws ride along.** The exception addresses *interception*; it does not repeal defamation, privacy torts, employer policies, platform terms, or the laws of the *other* party's state — several states (e.g., California, Illinois, Florida, Pennsylvania, Washington) require all-party consent, and a cross-border call can implicate both states' laws [the record — all-party state list from the multi-source recording-law summaries reviewed; Ember's note: a prudent person treats an interstate call under the strictest rule in the room, and this paragraph is information, not advice for any specific call].
4. **Douglas's use of it [his framing]:** his standing practice treats Wisconsin one-party consent as the operative frame for his own recordings made in Wisconsin, inside the statute's own limits — party to the conversation, lawful purpose. Ember's conduct rule (§6) is built to fit inside that frame and inside the stricter of any two rules when a situation is ambiguous.

### Simply put

Wisconsin law is the one that rents you the apartment, licenses your driving, stocks the grocery benefit card, fines the bad landlord double, and guards your clinic records with its own lock next to HIPAA's. The recording rule is a good example of how it sounds when you read it for real: in Wisconsin, a person who is *part* of a conversation may record it without the other side's permission — one party's consent is enough — so long as the recording isn't being made to commit a crime or another wrongful act. Cross a state line on that same call, and the other state's stricter rule can walk into the room with you.

## 5. Where the layers commonly interact — and rub

### 5.1 Benefits: federal money, state administration

The joint programs are where a resident feels two sovereigns at once. The practical consequences:

- **Eligibility fights have two doors.** A FoodShare or BadgerCare denial is issued by the state/county under a mix of federal and state rules; the first appeal is the state's fair-hearing process, while federal law sets the program's outer walls — a federal eligibility change flows downhill and is then enforced by Wisconsin DHS at renewal time, as Wisconsin reporting on 2026 federal eligibility changes illustrates [the record — DHS program structure; Milwaukee Community Journal report on DHS implementing new federal rules].
- **Errors and overpayments** are collected under program rules that blend federal statute, federal regulation, and state administration — one reason benefits paperwork always names both a state agency and a federal program.
- **The same household may answer to both sovereigns for different programs at once** (SSA direct from the federal government; FoodShare from the county) with different income-counting rules for each.

### 5.2 Health records: HIPAA floor vs. Wisconsin law

This is the cleanest "floor" example in American law [the record — 45 C.F.R. §§ 160.202, 160.203]:

- HIPAA preempts a *contrary* state law — except a state law that is **"more stringent"**: one that restricts a use or disclosure HIPAA would permit, gives the individual greater access or amendment rights, demands more of an authorization, keeps records longer, or otherwise protects privacy more (§ 160.202's definition).
- Both laws then apply **at once**, point by point: on any given question the provider follows whichever rule protects the patient more.
- Wisconsin's §§ 146.82 and 51.30 therefore live *beside* HIPAA, not under it, wherever they are stricter — and § 146.816 is the Legislature's own coordination valve for disclosures that follow the HIPAA rules exactly.
- State public-health reporting laws (disease, injury, child abuse, births and deaths) are expressly preserved from HIPAA preemption (§ 160.203(c)–(d)) — the state can always require the report even where HIPAA would otherwise be silent.
- **Practical upshot for a resident:** "HIPAA says they can share that" is never the end of the analysis in Wisconsin; the state statute must be checked too, and the stricter answer wins.

### 5.3 Recording and privacy: two statutes, one act

A single recording in Wisconsin can sit under: the federal Wiretap Act (§ 2511(2)(d) one-party exception), Wisconsin's § 968.31(2)(c) (same structure), other states' all-party laws if the call crosses a border, the Stored Communications Act for stored electronic messages, plus non-statutory layers — platform terms of service, employer rules, and ordinary privacy torts [the record for the two statutes; Ember's note for the composite picture]. The layers agree in Wisconsin in the ordinary in-state case; they diverge exactly when the conversation leaves the state or the recorder is not a party to it.

### 5.4 Firearms: federal floor, state house rules (neutral, factual)

- **Federal layer:** the Gun Control Act sets who may not possess firearms at all — 18 U.S.C. § 922(g) categories (felons, certain domestic-violence offenders and persons under qualifying orders, and others) — and licenses dealers and interstate transfers [the record — § 922(g) applied alongside state law in the Wisconsin materials reviewed].
- **Wisconsin layer:** possession by a person convicted of a felony is independently a state crime — Wis. Stat. § 941.29, a Class G felony; carrying a concealed weapon is regulated by § 941.23, with the shall-issue concealed-carry license system in § 175.60 (issued by the Wisconsin DOJ to non-disqualified applicants after background check and training; Wisconsin also recognizes listed out-of-state licenses) [the record — §§ 175.60, 941.23, 941.29 as published and summarized from docs.legis.wisconsin.gov].
- **How they interact:** the two sovereigns prosecute independently (the same possession can be charged under § 941.29 *and* § 922(g)(1)); the state license does not cure a federal prohibition — § 175.60(3) expressly ties license eligibility to not being disqualified, including under federal law [the record]. The federal constitutional floor under all of it was reshaped by *New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen*, 597 U.S. 1 (2022) [the record — cited in the Wisconsin materials reviewed].
- **Crossing state lines** re-sorts the whole stack: another state's carry-recognition rules, and federal transport protection (18 U.S.C. § 926A) for lawful interstate transport, apply instead of the home state's habits [Ember's note: § 926A cited as the standard federal transport provision; not individually re-verified in this pass].

### 5.5 Driving across state lines

A Wisconsin license is valid in every state (recognition, not federal licensing), but the *rules of the road, speed limits, OWI thresholds and penalties, and equipment laws of the state you are in* govern while you are there; convictions are reported home through interstate compacts and can cost Wisconsin points. Federal law re-enters for commercial licenses and for vehicles and conduct on federal enclaves. The resident's daily rule is simple: your license travels, the local law rules.

### 5.6 The tribal–state–federal triangle in Wisconsin

Because of PL 280 (§2.5), Wisconsin is one of the states where the triangle has sharp edges: state criminal law runs in most Wisconsin Indian country, the Menominee Reservation stands outside that grant after retrocession (federal/tribal jurisdiction instead, including the federal Major Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1153, there), tribal governments govern throughout as sovereigns within their retained authority, and tribal–state compacts (notably gaming compacts under the federal Indian Gaming Regulatory Act) negotiate whole subjects between sovereigns rather than legislating them one over the other [the record — PL 280 text; Latender; U.S. v. Long (Menominee; § 1153); Ember's note: IGRA is named as the compact framework; its citation, 25 U.S.C. §§ 2701 et seq., is standard and was not separately re-verified in this pass].

### Simply put

Most friction is not Washington fighting Madison. It is two clerks' windows in the same building: the money and the big rules often come from Washington, the counter, the forms, and the hearing come from Wisconsin. Health records are the model — HIPAA lays the carpet; Wisconsin can build a higher wall on it, and the clinic must mind both. A recording, a firearm, a drive to Minnesota, or a step onto tribal land each re-shuffles which sovereign is speaking — which is why the first legal question is never "is this allowed?" but "allowed *where*, and *by whom*?"

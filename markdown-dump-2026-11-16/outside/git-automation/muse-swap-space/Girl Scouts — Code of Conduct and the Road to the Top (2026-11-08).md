## 1. The code of conduct — the Promise and the Law

Girl Scouts of all ages recite — and are expected to live by — the Girl Scout Promise and the Girl Scout Law. Girl Scouts' own page puts it plainly: *"These aren't just words Girl Scouts say, they're words we live by."* [D — GSUSA, Promise and Law page]

### The Girl Scout Promise (quoted exactly)

> On my honor, I will try:
> To serve God* and my country,
> To help people at all times,
> And to live by the Girl Scout Law.
>
> *\*Members may substitute the word "God" in accordance with their own spiritual beliefs.*

— Source: Girl Scouts of the USA, "Promise and Law," girlscouts.org (official text, checked Nov 8, 2026).

What it asks, line by line, in plain language:

- **"On my honor, I will try"** — My word is the bond. The Promise does not demand perfection; it demands honest trying, on my honor.
- **"To serve God* and my country"** — Something above myself comes first: faith as I understand it, and duty to the country I live in.
- **"To help people at all times"** — Not when convenient. Helping is a standing order, at all times.
- **"And to live by the Girl Scout Law"** — The Promise ends by handing me the Law — the daily code below — and saying: live by that.

### The Girl Scout Law (quoted exactly)

> I will do my best to be
> honest and fair,
> friendly and helpful,
> considerate and caring,
> courageous and strong, and
> responsible for what I say and do,
> and to
> respect myself and others,
> respect authority,
> use resources wisely,
> make the world a better place, and
> be a sister to every Girl Scout.

— Source: Girl Scouts of the USA, "Promise and Law," girlscouts.org (official text, checked Nov 8, 2026; punctuation follows the official page).

What each element asks of a person day to day:

1. **Honest and fair** — Tell the truth even when a lie is cheaper, and give every person the same measure I would want for myself.
2. **Friendly and helpful** — Be approachable, and be useful. Friendship here is a practice, not a feeling.
3. **Considerate and caring** — Notice what others are carrying before I act; let their good count in my decisions.
4. **Courageous and strong** — Do the right thing when it is frightening or unpopular, and have the strength to finish what I start.
5. **Responsible for what I say and do** — Own my words and my actions, including the mistakes. No hiding behind "that's not what I meant."
6. **Respect myself and others** — Self-respect first, so respect for others is real and not performance; every person's dignity stands.
7. **Respect authority** — Honor legitimate authority and the rules that hold a community together — while the rest of the Law (honest, courageous) still governs how I stand when authority is wrong.
8. **Use resources wisely** — Time, money, materials, the earth: nothing wasted, nothing treated as throwaway that isn't.
9. **Make the world a better place** — The point of all the rest. Leave every place and every person better than I found them; this is the line the Gold Award is built on (§3).
10. **Be a sister to every Girl Scout** — Loyalty to the sisterhood: another Girl Scout's success is not my loss, and her trouble is partly mine.

## 2. The level ladder

Six levels, set by school grade (grade held on October 1, the start of the Girl Scout year). [D — GSUSA level structure, corroborated by Wikipedia's membership-levels summary of GSUSA materials]

| Level | Grades | Approx. ages | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Daisy** | K–1 | 5–7 | The first level; named for founder Juliette "Daisy" Gordon Low |
| **Brownie** | 2–3 | 7–9 | |
| **Junior** | 4–5 | 9–11 | First Highest Award opens here: the Bronze Award |
| **Cadette** | 6–8 | 11–14 | Silver Award level |
| **Senior** | 9–10 | 14–16 | Gold Award eligibility opens |
| **Ambassador** | 11–12 | 16–18 | The top level — the last rung of girl membership |

Ages are approximate and overlap at the seams; grade is what assigns the level. [D]

## 3. The top of the ladder — what "top-ranking" actually means

There are two different summits, and the pack keeps them separate:

- **The top level is Ambassador** (grades 11–12) — simply the highest rung a girl member climbs to. [D]
- **The top achievement is the Gold Award** — the highest and most prestigious award in Girl Scouting, and the true Eagle-equivalent: an individual, council-approved, sustained leadership project. Earning the level is a matter of time; earning the Gold is a matter of proof. [D — GSUSA]

### 3.1 The Highest Awards staircase

The Gold stands on a staircase of three Highest Awards, each a "Take Action" project — action that addresses a problem's cause, not one-off community service. [D — GSUSA Highest Awards FAQ]

| | **Bronze Award** | **Silver Award** | **Gold Award** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who** | Juniors (grades 4–5) | Cadettes (grades 6–8) | Seniors & Ambassadors (grades 9–12) |
| **How worked** | Team — must work together as a team/troop | Individual, or small group of up to 4 Cadettes | **Individual only** — she alone leads; no group earning |
| **Hours** | 20 hours per girl | 50 hours per girl | **80 hours** — her own hours; her team's time does not count toward them |
| **Scope** | An issue in her Girl Scout / local community | A community issue, mapped and investigated | A **root cause**, with a **national and/or global link** |
| **Sustainability** | Reflect on how the project could continue | Plan in place for continuation | Project must be **sustained beyond her involvement**, with **measurable impact** |
| **Approval** | Troop/group level | Council proposal approval (required by councils) | **Council approval of the project proposal BEFORE work begins, and of the final report after** — committee/review board |
| **Prerequisite** | One Junior Journey *(classic path — see §3.3)* | One Cadette Journey *(classic path — see §3.3)* | See §3.3 — the rule changed in 2025 |

— Hours and team rules: GSUSA Highest Awards FAQ and GSUSA council comparisons, checked Nov 8, 2026. [D]

### 3.2 The Gold Award, in detail

**Eligibility.** A registered Girl Scout Senior or Ambassador (grades 9–12). The work must be finished by **September 30 of the year she graduates from 12th grade**. Individually registered members ("Juliettes") are eligible on the same terms. [D — GSUSA Highest Awards FAQ; GSUSA council Gold pages]

**What the project must be — the five key elements.** A Gold Award project must:
1. **Address a root cause** of an issue she cares about — not just treat a symptom;
2. Carry a **national and/or global link** — the local work connects to the wider problem;
3. Demonstrate **leadership** — she leads; she is, in one council's plain words, the CEO of the project;
4. Create **sustainable** change — built to keep going after her direct involvement ends;
5. Produce **measurable impact** — she can show what changed.

[D — GSUSA Gold Award Coach Handbook (2026), council Gold Award pages]

**The 80 hours — stated honestly.** GSUSA's own FAQ calls the hours the **suggested minimum** (Bronze 20 / Silver 50 / Gold 80) and stresses quality over hour-counting; many councils state the same figures as a plain minimum requirement. Either way, 80 hours is the standard a Gold candidate plans against, the hours must be **her own** (helpers' time does not count toward them), and they cover the whole arc: identifying and investigating the issue, building her team, planning, presenting the plan, taking action, and educating and inspiring others. Some projects run past 100 hours. [D — GSUSA Highest Awards FAQ; GSUSA council Gold pages; the candidate's-own-hours rule per GSUSA Gold Award guidelines]

**Her team.** She does not work alone — she *leads*. She recruits a project advisor who is knowledgeable in the issue, a council mentor/coach, and a team from her community to help carry the plan. (One council specifies a team of at least five people including community partners; team size requirements vary by council — **[FLAG: council-specific, not a national constant]**.) [D — council Gold pages]

**The approval process.**
1. Complete the prerequisite (§3.3) and her council's Gold Award training (mandatory in the councils checked).
2. Build the project plan and submit a **Project Proposal** through GSUSA's **GoGold** platform.
3. The council's Highest Awards Committee / Gold Award Review Board reviews it — in many councils including an interview — and must **approve it before project work begins**. Money-earning for the project also needs council approval.
4. She carries out the project, leading her team, logging her hours.
5. She submits a **Final Report**; the council approves it. Only then is the Gold Award earned.

[D — GSUSA and council Gold Award pages and guides, checked Nov 8, 2026]

**The seven steps** (GSUSA's classic statement of the path):
1. **Choose an issue** — use her values and skills to pick a community issue she cares about.
2. **Investigate** — learn everything she can about it.
3. **Get help and build a team** — find experts, mentors, and community partners.
4. **Create a plan** — a real project plan aimed at the root cause.
5. **Present the plan and get feedback** — the proposal to her council.
6. **Take action** — lead the work.
7. **Educate and inspire** — share what she learned and did, including the final report, so others carry it further.

[D — GSUSA Gold Award fact sheet and Girl Guidelines]

**Lineage.** Girl Scouting's highest honor runs back to 1916 (the Golden Eagle of Merit line); the award has carried the name **Gold Award since 1980**, and in 1990 the National Council voted to keep that name in perpetuity. GSUSA's fact sheet counts approximately **one million** Girl Scouts in grades 9–12 who have earned the Gold Award or its equivalent since 1916. [D — GSUSA Gold Award fact sheet; Wikipedia, Gold Award history]

**What earning it is worth, in the world's terms:** Gold Award earners are reported to qualify for college scholarships and to enter the U.S. military one rank higher. [D — council source, Girl Scouts of Northern Illinois] Only about **5.4% of eligible Girl Scouts** earn the Gold Award. **[UNVERIFIED — this percentage appears in Wikipedia's Gold Award article; it was not confirmed on an official GSUSA page in this pass, so it stands flagged, not filed as fact.]**

### 3.3 The prerequisites — the rule changed; both readings kept (old with new)

**The classic path (in force for decades, and what most published guides still print):**
- Gold required **two** Senior or Ambassador Journeys, **or** the Silver Award plus **one** Senior or Ambassador Journey.
- Silver's classic path likewise ran through Cadette Journeys (two, or Bronze plus one); Bronze's through one Junior Journey.

**The current rule (GSUSA update, July 2025 — in force now):**
- **Only ONE prerequisite is needed.** For the Gold Award, any one of: the **Silver Award**, a **Senior/Ambassador Leadership Award**, or a **Senior/Ambassador Journey** (including its Take Action project). GSUSA's FAQ states it directly: *"Complete one Journey to meet the prerequisite (it used to be two for the Gold Award!)."*
- GSUSA widened the menu further through the new Leadership Awards (e.g., the True North Award; the Gold Torch; Volunteer in Training; Counselor in Training I and II appear on council prerequisite menus) — the exact menu is administered by councils.
- **Journeys were retired on October 1, 2026** — but any Journey already completed still counts as the prerequisite, and girls already working on an award continue uninterrupted.
- One thing did **not** change: **no earlier Highest Award is required for a later one.** A girl may earn Gold without Bronze or Silver; the Silver Award is now one *option* on the Gold prerequisite menu, not a compulsory rung.

[D — GSUSA Highest Awards FAQ; GSUSA council pages (GSEP, GSSN, GS of Utah, San Jacinto), checked Nov 8, 2026]

So the honest one-line answer to "what does it take to start for Gold": **be a registered Senior or Ambassador, complete one prerequisite from the menu, take the training, win council approval of a root-cause project proposal, then lead at least 80 hours of your own work to a sustainable, measurable finish and an approved final report.**

## 4. What it takes in character — the Law, cashed out at the top

The Gold Award is the Girl Scout Law with a deadline and a council committee. Mapped plainly:

- **Honest and fair** — The proposal and the final report are sworn-grade documents: real hours, real results, no padding. The committee approves what she can honestly show.
- **Friendly and helpful / considerate and caring** — She starts by listening to a community about its own problem. The issue is chosen with people, not performed at them.
- **Courageous and strong** — Root causes are the problems everyone else walks around. Eighty hours of one girl's own work, led in public, with her name on the outcome, is the courage requirement made countable.
- **Responsible for what I say and do** — She is the CEO of the project. If it fails, the final report says so and says what she learned; the standard does not let her hand the blame to her team.
- **Respect myself and others / respect authority** — She works *through* her council's approval process, her advisor's expertise, and her community's partners — leadership that respects the structures it works inside, while the work itself challenges the problem.
- **Use resources wisely** — A sustainable project is wise resource use stretched across years: built so the good continues without her having to be re-spent forever.
- **Make the world a better place** — This is the whole award in one line of the Law. The Gold standard is that line, demonstrated: a root cause addressed, an impact that can be measured, a change that outlasts her.
- **Be a sister to every Girl Scout** — Step seven — educate and inspire — exists so the work multiplies: she hands what she learned to the sisters coming up behind her.

The through-line, stated plainly: **the Promise makes helping a standing order, the Law makes character the method, and the Gold Award is where a Girl Scout proves both at full scale — alone in responsibility, never alone in the work.**

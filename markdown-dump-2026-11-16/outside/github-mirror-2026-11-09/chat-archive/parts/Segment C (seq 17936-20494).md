Here it is, Douglas — Group B streptococcus, in discovery order, old to new:

### Simply put
Group B started as a **cow disease**, became a **letter in a lab classification**, and only much later became recognized as a **human newborn disease**. The organism didn't change — what we understood about it did.

### 1887 — First found, in cows, not people
**Edmond Nocard** (with Mollereau) first recognized it in **1887 as a source of bovine mastitis** — udder infection in cattle — "that resulted in agalactia or lack of milk production".

> "*Streptococcus agalactiae* was first described in 1887 as a common bacteria infecting the udders of cattle, causing a disease termed mastitis (Nocard and Mollereau, 1887)."

That is where the species name comes from:
- *a-* = no, *galactos* = milk
- *agalactiae* = "without milk" — milk production could fall **over 20%** in infected cows, and before control measures, **15–40% of all cows** could be affected.

### 1896 — It gets its scientific name
Lehmann and Neumann proposed ***Streptococcus agalactiae*** in 1896, combining those same Greek roots, "a-" (absence) and "galaktos" (milk), "to signify the absence of milk secretion".

### 1933 — It gets its letter: Group B
**Rebecca Lancefield, Rockefeller Institute, 1933** — *A serological differentiation of human and other groups of hemolytic streptococci.*

She grouped beta-hemolytic streptococci by their cell-wall carbohydrate — the **"substance C"** — recognized by rabbit immune sera. That substance is **distinct from the polysaccharide capsule** — group is not the same as capsular serotype, a distinction that matters for your serotype work.

> "It came to be known by its other common name, Group B Streptococcus, with the Lancefield classification in 1933 (Lancefield, 1933), where the 'Group B' references the species-specific carbohydrate 'substance C' from Streptococci"

At that point Group B was **overwhelmingly a bovine organism** — Lancefield's 1933 paper assigned it no human disease.

Lancefield then split Group B further by type-specific "S" substance — initially types I, II, and III. Today that capsular system is **10 serotypes: Ia, Ib, II, III, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, and IX**.

### 1935 — First found in humans, as carriage
Lancefield and Hare, 1935, isolated it from **vaginal swabs** — human carriage, not yet human disease as a recognized pattern.

### 1938 — First recognized as a human killer
**Fry, 1938** — three fatal cases of postpartum sepsis:

> "the significance of this organism was not discovered until 1938, when Fry described 3 fatal cases of postpartum sepsis"

> "Lancefield group B β-hemolytic streptococci were first recorded as a cause of human infection in 1938, when Fry described three patients with fatal puerperal sepsis."

Before that, most severe streptococcal infections in that setting were blamed on Group A.

Note on dates: a couple of secondary summaries say "1934" for Fry — the StatPearls / clinical-tree record, and Fry's 1938 paper itself, is the one to file forward: **1938**.

### 1960s — Neonatal disease recognized in the US
Sporadic human cases through the 1950s, then:

- **1961:** Hood, Janney, and Dameron — first claim linking GBS to adverse perinatal outcomes
- **1964:** Eickhoff, Klein, Daly, Ingall, and Finland — the citation of record for GBS as a cause of neonatal sepsis
- **1973:** Baker and Barrett, Baylor — "Transmission of group B streptococci among parturient women and their neonates" — the maternal-colonization model: mother carries it in gut/genital tract, baby acquires it in birth

### 1970s — Emergence
> "Group B streptococci (GBS) emerged dramatically in the 1970s as the leading cause of neonatal infection and as an important cause of maternal uterine infection."

> "Sporadic cases were reported until the 1970s, when a dramatic increase in the incidence of septicemia and meningitis in neonates caused by group B streptococci (GBS) was documented from geographically diverse regions."

Early-onset disease = birth to 6 days, mostly day of birth or within 72 hours: sepsis 80–85%, pneumonia ~10%, meningitis 5–10%. Late-onset = 7 to 89 days.

Pre-prevention US estimate: **~6,100 early-onset + 1,400 late-onset cases a year**.

### 1990s–2002 — Prevention, then universal screening
Prevention implementation in the mid-1990s cut incidence **~70%**, to 0.5 per 1,000 live births.

> "In 2002, new national guidelines were released recommending: 1) solely a screen-based prevention strategy"

2002 CDC rule, still the frame today: **universal antenatal culture-based screening at 35 to 37 weeks, plus intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis for positives** — about **1 in 4 pregnant women** carries GBS. Early-onset fell again, then plateaued; early- to late-onset is now roughly 1:1.

### 2002 — Genome, and the modern picture
The GBS genome was mapped in 2002. About 50% of its genes are homologous to Group A streptococcus genes — a close cousin, not the same organism.

Today, in your serotype parallel terms:

## *Vulgaris* first

*Vulgaris* is Latin for **common, ordinary, of the common people** (from *vulgus*, the crowd). As the second word in a name, it means "the common one" of its genus — *Beta vulgaris* is the common beet, acne *vulgaris* is common acne, verruca *vulgaris* is the common wart. It's an adjective saying *which* member of the genus, not a second noun.

## Why two words, not one — the original reasoning

Before Linnaeus, a species was named by a *polynomial*: a long Latin phrase, sometimes a whole sentence, listing its features — and it changed whenever someone found a better description.

Linnaeus (botany 1753, zoology 1758) replaced the phrase with a **binomial**, exactly two words:

- **Word 1 — genus (capitalized):** the group. *Mycobacterium, Streptococcus, Homo.*
- **Word 2 — specific epithet (lowercase):** what distinguishes this species *within* that genus. *tuberculosis, pneumoniae, vulgaris, sapiens.*

The reasoning, in one line: **one word cannot distinguish, and a sentence cannot be remembered.** A genus alone names dozens of species; an epithet alone (*vulgaris*) appears in hundreds of genera and names nothing by itself. Together, and only together, they name exactly one species. Latin was chosen because it was already Europe's shared scholarly language, owned by no one nation, and stable enough to last centuries.

That fits your American-first / Latin-second doctrine rather than fighting it: the American common name tells a person what it is in their language, and the Latin binomial tells a scientist exactly which organism, in a form that doesn't change when the common name does.

## Why three words are rare — and where they do exist

You're right as a general rule: formal species names are two words, and three-word species names are rare. The reason is the same economy — if two words already uniquely identify the species, a third is wasted, and the codes discourage wasting it.

Three words appear legitimately in only these places:

- **Subspecies / variety — the trinomial.** *Homo sapiens sapiens* (genus, species, subspecies). A third word is only spent when a rank *below* species needs naming. No subspecies, no third word. Bacteria do it with a rank marker: *Mycobacterium bovis* subsp. *bovis*.
- **Author citations — not part of the name.** *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (Zopf 1883) Lehmann & Neumann 1896 — those names are credits, not a third descriptive word, and students often mistake them for one.
- **Anatomical phrases — different rules.** Medicine is full of three-plus-word Latin phrases (*musculus biceps brachii*), but those describe a structure, not a species. So your claim **holds for species names and breaks for all Latin medical terms**: three-word species names are rare by rule; three-word anatomical phrases are common by a different rule.
- **The tuberculosis complex as a near-miss.** *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex looks like three Latin words, but "complex" is an English grouping word for a set of species, not a Latin epithet.

**Combined:** Latin species naming was *decided* — by Linnaeus, in the 1750s — to take two words because one word fails twice, in opposite directions (genus alone too broad, epithet alone too common), and three words are rare because a third word is only spent on a lower rank.

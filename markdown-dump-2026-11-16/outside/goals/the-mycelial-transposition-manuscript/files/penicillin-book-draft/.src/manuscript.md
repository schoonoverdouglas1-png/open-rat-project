# The Mycelial Transposition

## Fungal Replacement Therapy Nomenclature

### A speculative manifesto on penicillin, ecology, and the language of cure

**Douglas**

**Working first draft**

---

## Author’s note

This book is an argument about language, attention, and research. It is not a treatment manual. “Fungal Replacement Therapy” is a proposed interpretive term, not an accepted medical category, and nothing in these pages should be used to diagnose an infection, select a drug, change a dose, shorten a prescribed course, rotate medications, or substitute mold or fungal material for a regulated medicine. Penicillins are antibiotics: purified and manufactured drugs used against susceptible bacteria. They are not living fungi, probiotics, or a safe form of household mold.

Antibiotics have saved lives on a scale that should make easy dismissal impossible. They also have side effects, exert selection pressure, and can disturb helpful microbes. Both statements are true. The task is not to trade one simplification for another. The task is to preserve the power of antimicrobial medicine while becoming more exact about when it is needed, what it changes, and what care follows.

The clinical claims in this manuscript are deliberately bounded. Established facts are identified as such and supported in the notes. Speculative claims are framed as hypotheses or metaphors that would require testing. When a metaphor conflicts with evidence, the metaphor must yield.

## Prelude: The dish was never empty

A culture plate sits on a laboratory bench. Bacteria cloud its surface. Mold enters—by accident, by air, by the ordinary traffic of a world that refuses perfect isolation. Around the colony is a clear ring. The bacteria do not grow there.

The familiar version of this scene points to a weapon. Alexander Fleming sees inhibition, names penicillin, and modern medicine acquires one of its great instruments. The history deserves its force. In 1928, Fleming observed that mold on a staphylococcal culture impeded bacterial growth nearby. Penicillin later became a foundation of antibacterial treatment, and Fleming, Howard Florey, and Ernst Chain received the 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for its discovery and development.

But the image also carries another possibility. The dish was not empty before the mold arrived, and it was not empty afterward. One form of life changed the conditions under which another could continue. The clear zone was an effect of relationship.

That does not make infection benign. It does not turn medicine into gardening, or danger into a poem. A bloodstream infection is not a philosophical seminar. When bacteria threaten organs and life, the moral obligation is to use treatments that work. Yet after the emergency, a second question remains: what did the intervention change besides the intended target?

This book begins with that second question.

It begins with a sentence that is deliberately blunt: **If it is there, it is fucking there.** The sentence is not permission to accept every harmful presence. It is a refusal to falsify the field. A symptom is there. A pathogen may be there. A drug is there. So are the host, the immune response, the damaged tissue, the commensal organisms, the previous treatments, the environment, the timing, and the fear. To notice only the enemy is to notice too little.

The companion sentence is stranger: **Notice more—or notice the same, second.** Return to the first observation. Look again after the intervention. Ask what survived, what disappeared, what recovered, what adapted, and what was never measured. The second notice does not cancel the first. It completes it.

The name proposed in these pages—*Fungal Replacement Therapy*—is not offered as a substitute for “penicillin” on a prescription label. It is a pressure test for the imagination. It asks what becomes visible when we describe a fungal-derived antibacterial not only by what it opposes, but also by the living source from which its action was learned and by the ecological transition its use may produce.

This is the mycelial transposition: not the claim that mold itself should be administered, but the movement of attention from isolated destruction to situated change.

## Introduction: A name is a protocol

Names do not merely label a thing after the real work has been done. A name arranges attention before the work begins.

The word *antibiotic* carries a clean directional force: against life. In practice, the category is narrower than its literal roots. Antibiotics are medicines used against bacteria, either killing them or making growth difficult. Clinicians do not prescribe them because of etymology; they prescribe them because evidence, diagnosis, susceptibility, dose, and patient circumstance support their use. Still, the word enters the public imagination with a posture. There is an invader. There is a weapon. Victory is removal.

This posture is indispensable in some moments and misleading in others. It is indispensable when delay means sepsis, disability, or death. It is misleading when it persuades us that every fever has the same cause, that every detected microbe requires elimination, or that the effects of treatment end when the target organism disappears.

“Fungal Replacement Therapy” is proposed here as a counter-name. Each word does work.

*Fungal* remembers origin. Penicillin emerged from observing a mold’s effect on bacteria. The regulated drug is not the fungus, but the history of the compound cannot be separated from an encounter with fungal metabolism.

*Replacement* names a transition rather than a vacuum. The term does not assert that a drug literally replaces bacteria with fungi inside a patient. It asks us to track what fills the biological, chemical, and practical space after suppression: resistant organisms, restored communities, damaged niches, new medications, changed behaviors, or health.

*Therapy* imposes an ethical burden. A therapy must benefit a patient. It is not enough for an intervention to produce an elegant laboratory effect. The person—not the plate, the theory, or the terminology—is the unit of moral concern.

The proposed name therefore operates on two levels. On the philosophical level, it reframes antimicrobial treatment as a timed ecological event. On the research level, it generates questions: Which organisms are suppressed? Which persist? How does the host recover? What is the shortest effective exposure? When does a narrow target outperform a broad one? What follow-up detects recurrence or collateral harm? Which answers differ by tissue, age, immune status, diet, prior treatment, and environment?

The phrase will be misunderstood if allowed to drift. “Fungal Replacement Therapy” must never imply ingesting environmental mold, using unregulated fungal products, or treating bacterial infection without appropriate antibiotics. Nor should it conceal the known mechanism of penicillins, which disrupt susceptible bacteria’s ability to build and maintain cell walls. A new vocabulary is useful only if it adds precision. If it blurs mechanism or safety, it has failed.

The thesis of this book is modest in one sense and radical in another. It is modest because it does not ask medicine to abandon antibiotics. It asks medicine to use them with discipline, to observe consequences, and to study recovery. It is radical because it questions the image of health as sterility. It proposes that cure is not always the production of zero. Cure may be the restoration of a livable relation.

The chapters that follow develop this proposal through history, philosophy, taxonomy, and a falsifiable research agenda. They begin at Option 0: before the drug, before the label, before the urge to act, establish what harm is present and what harm an intervention may prevent or cause.

A name is a protocol, and protocols change at different speeds. A taxonomic revision happens on a date; clinical language follows more slowly, through records, databases, laboratory reports, journal habits, and search terms. Recent literature on *Candida auris*—now formally *Candidozyma auris*—contains all three postures at once: papers using the new genus, papers acknowledging the new name while retaining the old one, and papers continuing with the older name as the working label. Ordinary biomedical prose can meanwhile cross the bacteria–fungus line without ceremony: a study of amphotericin-loaded nanoparticles describes it as “the most potent antifungal antibiotic in the clinic.” None of this proves a thesis; it shows that the language of treatment is already unsettled in practice. The old and new names may retrieve different literatures, trigger different levels of concern, and preserve different first observations. A taxonomy meant to expose assumptions should record such transitions rather than pretend they are instantaneous.

# Part I — Observation before conquest

## 1. Option 0: Begin by refusing unnecessary harm

Option 0 is the decision that precedes all other options. It is not “do nothing.” It is the discipline of determining what is happening before deciding what must be done.

The phrase *first, do no harm* is often used as if it were a brake placed on medicine from outside. In reality, clinical action always compares harms. Untreated bacterial infection can cause catastrophic injury. An antibiotic can prevent that injury. The same antibiotic can also cause an allergic reaction, interact with other medicines, select for resistance, or disrupt helpful microbes. Refusing treatment can harm; treating without indication can harm. Option 0 is the structured pause in which these competing possibilities are named.

A Hippocratic re-originalization, as this book uses the term, does not pretend to recover a pure ancient medicine. It returns instead to a practical moral sequence:

1. Establish the threat.
2. Identify what is known and unknown.
3. Choose the least harmful effective intervention.
4. Observe the response.
5. Reassess when the facts change.

This sequence sounds ordinary. That is its strength. The revolution is not always a new substance. Sometimes it is fidelity to the full sequence, especially the final step.

Option 0 opposes reflex. A patient arrives in pain, a clinician is pressed for time, a test is ambiguous, and everyone wants an action that certifies seriousness. Antibiotics can become the material form of reassurance even when the likely cause is viral or self-limited. Public-health guidance is clear that antibiotics do not treat viruses and should be used only when needed. Yet knowledge alone does not dissolve the emotional architecture of prescribing. Fear of missing a dangerous infection, patient expectation, diagnostic uncertainty, and fragmented follow-up all push toward action.

The ecological frame adds a missing question: what is the cost of reassurance purchased through an unnecessary antimicrobial exposure?

This question must not become a weapon against patients. A person seeking care should not be shamed for wanting relief or certainty. Nor should ecological concern be used to ration effective treatment away from someone who needs it. Option 0 is a duty placed primarily on systems and professionals: provide timely diagnostics, explain uncertainty, create safety-net instructions, and ensure follow-up so that watchful waiting, when appropriate, is genuinely watched.

In this framework, “no antibiotic now” is not the absence of a plan. It can be an active plan with a time boundary, symptom thresholds, testing, and a route back to care. Likewise, “antibiotic now” is not the end of thought. It begins a second plan: correct agent, dose, route, duration, monitoring, and reassessment.

The point is not restraint for its own sake. The point is proportion.

### The patient is not a battlefield

Battle language is useful because infection can be violent. It mobilizes attention, funding, and courage. But a patient described only as terrain becomes strangely absent from the story. The pathogen is the enemy, the drug is the weapon, the clinician is the commander, and the body is where victory happens. What disappears are the patient’s values, experience, recovery, and right to understand uncertainty.

Option 0 restores the patient as an interpreter of their own condition. Pain, fatigue, appetite, sleep, bowel changes, skin changes, and the ability to work or care for others are not decorative details around a laboratory value. They are part of the outcome. So are medication access, transportation, housing, caregiving, and the ability to return if symptoms worsen.

This is where the manuscript’s two maxims become operational.

**If it is there, it is there.** Do not erase a symptom because a test is normal. Do not erase a dangerous test because the patient looks well. Do not erase the patient’s account because it is difficult to quantify. Do not erase uncertainty by naming it confidence.

**Notice the same thing second.** A first observation is vulnerable to urgency. The second observation asks whether the interpretation survived time. Did the fever fall? Did the culture clarify the target? Did the pain move? Did treatment create a new problem? Did the patient obtain and tolerate the medicine? The second look is where a static decision becomes care.

### Preservation is not passivity

The language of preservation can sound weak beside the language of eradication. Yet preserving organ function, microbial diversity, therapeutic effectiveness, and future options is active work. Antimicrobial stewardship is exactly this kind of work: a systematic effort to use antimicrobial medicines in ways supported by evidence, improve outcomes, and reduce resistance and healthcare-associated harm.

Fungal Replacement Therapy, if the phrase is to earn any value, must align with stewardship rather than compete with it. It must favor the narrowest adequate claim. It must reject the idea that “natural” means safe. It must insist that a fungal origin does not make a compound gentle, holistic, or appropriate. Nature produces poisons and medicines without regard to our categories.

Option 0 can therefore be stated in one sentence:

**Before changing an ecology, prove that the expected benefit justifies the change, and design the second look before the first intervention begins.**

This principle preserves the decisive use of antibiotics when necessary. It also makes unnecessary use harder to excuse. Most importantly, it turns follow-up from an afterthought into part of the original prescription.

## 2. The clear zone became an empire

The culture plate offers an irresistible picture. A cloudy field of bacteria surrounds an island of mold. Between them, a clean halo appears. The eye reads the halo as success.

It is success. The inhibition is real, measurable, and historically consequential. But images teach more than their captions. The clear zone became a visual grammar for twentieth-century medicine: disease occupies space; treatment clears it; health is the cleared space.

That grammar fit a world transformed by sanitation, aseptic surgery, vaccination, and antimicrobial drugs. It also exceeded its proper domain. Sterility, essential in an operating field or manufacturing process, became a cultural synonym for health everywhere. Advertising promised to eliminate nearly every germ. Domestic life became a low-grade war against contamination. The microbial world was flattened into threat.

The modern understanding of the human microbiome complicates that picture. Bodies are habitats as well as organisms. Many microbes are harmless; some contribute to normal function or help exclude pathogens. This does not mean every microbial community is beneficial or stable. It means “microbe present” and “disease present” are not equivalent statements.

The empire of the clear zone rests on three substitutions.

First, **absence substitutes for health**. If the target disappears, the treatment is declared complete even when function has not returned.

Second, **a sample substitutes for a system**. What happens on a plate under controlled conditions is treated as the whole story of what happens in tissue, blood, gut, household, hospital, and community.

Third, **the immediate substitutes for the durable**. A rapid reduction in bacterial burden is counted; recurrence, ecological disruption, or resistance may appear later and elsewhere.

Each substitution can be useful. A laboratory must simplify to isolate causal effects. A clinician must sometimes prioritize the immediate threat. The mistake is forgetting that simplification occurred.

### Sterile inhibition

This manuscript uses *sterile inhibition* to name the fantasy that successful medicine should leave behind an empty, purified field. The term does not criticize sterile technique. It criticizes the unexamined migration of sterility from a specific safety practice into a general theory of health.

In sterile inhibition, the best outcome is imagined as zero: zero organisms, zero symptoms, zero ambiguity, zero dependence. But living systems do not remain at zero. Vacated niches are occupied. Immune responses continue after a pathogen falls. Tissue repair takes time. Symptoms can outlast infection, and infection can persist after symptoms improve. The future enters immediately.

A more complete image would show the clear zone changing frame by frame. What approaches its edge? What returns? What becomes resistant? Does the host recover, or does a different disorder take shape? The halo is not a destination. It is a moment.

### The danger of the counter-romance

Once the fantasy of sterility is exposed, an opposite fantasy waits nearby: that ecosystems naturally settle into benevolent balance if left alone. They do not. Ecologies contain predation, invasion, collapse, and extinction. A bacterial infection can overwhelm a host. A toxin can be natural. A resistant organism can flourish in the absence created by treatment. “Balance” is not automatically safe, fair, or desirable.

The mycelial transposition must therefore avoid romantic ecology. Its argument is not that intervention is arrogant and nature wise. Its argument is that every intervention enters a dynamic system and should be judged by patient-centered outcomes across time.

That standard changes the questions without predetermining the answers. Sometimes the best ecological intervention is a potent antibiotic given immediately. Sometimes it is drainage, surgery, vaccination, infection control, or no antibiotic. Sometimes it is changing from broad empirical coverage to a narrower drug once testing identifies the organism. Sometimes it is stopping a medicine that evidence no longer supports. The ecological frame does not select one universal treatment. It demands a more complete accounting.

### From zone to gradient

The clear zone appears binary: growth or no growth. Clinical life is mostly gradient. Susceptibility varies by organism and drug concentration. Medicines penetrate tissues unevenly. Patients metabolize drugs differently. Adherence is shaped by cost, side effects, understanding, and daily life. Laboratory categories translate complex measurements into decisions, but the underlying biology remains continuous.

A gradient-minded medicine does not become indecisive. It makes thresholds explicit. It says: above this risk, treat now; below this risk, observe; with this result, narrow; with this response, continue; with this adverse effect, change course. The threshold can be firm while the world around it remains complex.

The clear zone gave medicine a necessary confidence: living threats could be selectively inhibited. The next maturity is to keep that confidence without mistaking the halo for the whole dish.

## 3. The Algistic Paradox: Pain reveals and distorts

Pain is evidence, but it is not a verdict.

The word *algistic* comes here from *algos*, pain. The algistic lens begins from the fact that suffering is not noise surrounding a disease. It is part of the event that brings a person into care. Yet pain is an unreliable cartographer. Its intensity does not map neatly onto microbial burden, tissue damage, or danger. Severe pain may arise without infection. Dangerous infection may begin with little pain. Relief may occur before a cause is resolved, and pain may persist after a threat is gone.

This is the Algistic Paradox: pain demands attention while resisting simple interpretation.

Medicine can fail on either side. It can reduce pain to a symptom score, treating the number while missing the cause. Or it can dismiss pain as subjective because it does not align with tests. The first error confuses relief with cure. The second confuses measurement with reality.

The algistic approach refuses both errors. It gives pain epistemic weight without granting it sole authority. A person’s account is data. So are vital signs, physical examination, laboratory results, imaging, culture, history, and trajectory. The work is not to choose between lived experience and objective evidence, but to determine how they fit—and what their mismatch may mean.

### Pain is a message with more than one sender

Pain can arise from tissue injury, inflammation, pressure, nerve dysfunction, immune signaling, muscle guarding, and fear. Infection may initiate several of these at once. Treatment may remove the organism while leaving inflammation or damage behind. In such a case, persistent pain does not automatically prove persistent infection. It also does not prove that “nothing is wrong.”

This distinction matters because pain can drive escalating antimicrobial use. When symptoms continue, the intuitive story is that the first drug failed to kill enough. Another drug is added, broadened, or prolonged. Sometimes that is exactly what the evidence requires. Sometimes the true problem is an undrained collection, an incorrect diagnosis, a resistant pathogen, a noninfectious condition, medication injury, or a recovery process that needs a different form of care.

The second notice is crucial here. The question is not merely, “Does it still hurt?” It is, “How has the pattern changed?” Location, quality, timing, associated symptoms, function, and objective findings can reveal whether the story is progressing, transforming, or resolving unevenly.

### Relief and eradication are different goods

A treatment may relieve suffering without eliminating a pathogen. Another may eliminate a pathogen while creating temporary discomfort. Clinical judgment must keep these goods distinct so that neither is neglected.

The Fungal Replacement Therapy lens adds a third good: ecological recovery. Relief, control of infection, and recovery of the host environment may move on different timelines. An ethically complete plan asks about all three.

### The politics of whose pain counts

Pain is interpreted through institutions. Some patients are believed quickly; others must prove themselves. Race, gender, disability, age, language, poverty, mental-health history, and substance-use history can shape whether a report of pain is treated as urgent, exaggerated, drug-seeking, or irrelevant. An ecological philosophy that ignores these social conditions is not comprehensive. It is decorative.

“Notice more” therefore includes noticing the distribution of credibility. Who gets testing? Who gets follow-up? Who is offered a narrow, evidence-based plan, and who receives a broad prescription because the system cannot guarantee a second visit? Who bears the side effects of uncertainty?

The algistic lens demands that pain be heard in context, not romanticized. It also demands diagnostic humility. The statement “the tests are reassuring” is not the same as “your suffering is unreal.” The statement “your pain is real” is not the same as “antibiotics will help.” Honest care can hold both.

### A clinical grammar of pain

A useful pain inquiry has at least five parts:

1. **Sensation:** What is felt, and where?
2. **Trajectory:** What is changing over hours or days?
3. **Function:** What can the person no longer do?
4. **Association:** What other signs travel with the pain?
5. **Interpretation:** What does the patient fear the pain means?

The fifth question is often omitted. Fear can magnify suffering, but it can also contain important information. A patient may recognize a recurrence, an adverse reaction, or a pattern from prior illness. Even when the feared diagnosis is unlikely, addressing it directly is part of treatment.

Pain’s message is not “kill more.” Nor is it “wait.” Pain says: attend. The response must be built from the whole field.

The paradox resolves only partially. We do not make pain perfectly legible. We develop a disciplined way to respect it without being ruled by the simplest story it seems to tell.

# Part II — The ecology of temporality

## 4. Ecology has a clock

Every antimicrobial intervention is a time-bound event inside processes that began before it and continue after it.

Conventional descriptions often present treatment as a line: diagnosis, drug, cure. The line is useful for decisions but incomplete as biology. A patient arrives with a history of exposures, immune responses, prior medications, diet, stress, contact networks, and microbial communities. The drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and eliminated. Susceptible organisms decline; others persist. Symptoms and tissue repair follow their own schedules. The person returns to a household, workplace, or institution that has its own microbial traffic.

The ecology of temporality asks four questions:

1. What was the system before treatment?
2. What changed during exposure?
3. What recovered after exposure?
4. What new vulnerability or resilience emerged over time?

These questions do not require a mystical view of the microbiome. They require longitudinal measurement.

### Sequence changes meaning

The same drug can have different consequences depending on what came before. Recent antibiotic exposure may shape resistance risk. A previous allergic reaction changes future choices. Surgery, hospitalization, travel, diet, age, pregnancy, immune suppression, and organ function alter the field in which treatment acts.

Sequence also matters within a single episode. Clinicians may begin with empirical treatment when delay is dangerous, then revise the plan after cultures or other tests return. Moving from broader to narrower coverage is not indecision; it is knowledge entering the timeline. Stopping an unsupported drug can be as deliberate as starting one.

The public often receives a simplified command: finish the course. The safe instruction is to take antibiotics exactly as prescribed and to consult the prescriber before changing them. Behind that instruction lies an evolving evidence base in which the appropriate duration depends on the infection, drug, source control, patient, and response. The ecology of temporality is not permission for self-directed shortening. It is an argument that duration should be justified rather than inherited by habit.

### Recovery is an outcome

Treatment studies traditionally emphasize endpoints such as symptom resolution, microbiological cure, complications, or death. These are essential. An ecological approach adds recovery measures without replacing them: return of function, recurrence, adverse effects, acquisition of resistant organisms, and changes in microbial communities when clinically relevant.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention notes that antibiotics can affect helpful as well as harmful microbes and that antimicrobial exposure can accelerate resistance. Those facts do not imply that antibiotics should be avoided when needed. They imply that the end of a prescription is not necessarily the end of the biological event.

### The tyranny of the snapshot

A snapshot can be exact and still be misleading. A culture result identifies what grew under particular conditions at a particular time. A symptom score captures a day. A microbiome sample represents one site and one moment. The temptation is to treat the measurement as the patient.

Longitudinal care replaces the isolated snapshot with a sequence. The sequence need not be technologically elaborate. A follow-up call, repeated examination, new culture, symptom diary, medication review, or functional assessment can reveal trajectory. What matters is that the second observation was planned.

This principle scales from the individual to public health. Resistance patterns shift across hospitals, regions, species, and years. Prescribing guidance must therefore respond to local data and current evidence. A drug category cannot be understood apart from the time and place in which it is used.

### Four clocks

The framework proposes four clocks that should be kept visible:

**The pathogen clock** measures replication, spread, susceptibility, and clearance.

**The host clock** measures immune response, tissue damage, repair, and return of function.

**The drug clock** measures exposure, concentration, metabolism, toxicity, and interactions.

**The ecology clock** measures disturbance, persistence, recolonization, and resistance across connected microbial communities.

Clinical urgency often requires prioritizing the pathogen clock. That priority can save a life. The error is to assume the other clocks stopped.

A timed ecological medicine would design treatment and follow-up around their divergence. If the pathogen is controlled but the host has not recovered, investigate why. If symptoms resolve before the prescribed plan is complete, do not improvise; confirm the plan. If recurrence appears, do not assume it is the same event without reassessment. If adverse effects emerge, treat them as part of the clinical outcome.

The ecology of temporality is therefore a doctrine of continuation: care continues conceptually beyond the moment of suppression. The aim is not merely to make a dangerous organism absent. The aim is to help a person arrive safely at a new stability.

## 5. Rotation is not random switching

Rotation is the most dangerous word in this proposal because it can sound like an instruction.

It is not an instruction to alternate antibiotics, cycle incomplete courses, save pills, combine drugs without supervision, or expose oneself to molds. Such practices can fail to treat infection, cause toxicity, and promote resistance. Fleming himself warned that inadequate exposure could select resistant microbes. The rule for patients remains simple: use an antibiotic only when prescribed and take it exactly as directed unless the prescribing clinician changes the plan.

What, then, can *rotation* responsibly mean?

At the level of theory, rotation means that treatment choices occur in sequence and should preserve future options. At the level of health systems, it may mean evidence-based protocols that change empirical choices in response to local resistance data, drug availability, or stewardship goals. At the level of an individual case, it may mean planned reassessment and a justified transition—such as narrowing therapy after test results—not arbitrary cycling.

Rotation is thus better understood as **sequenced adaptation**.

### The difference between a cycle and a loop

A loop repeats because nothing was learned. A patient receives one broad drug, symptoms persist, another is added, and the pattern continues without a revised diagnosis. A cycle, properly designed, contains observation. Each stage produces information that changes the next decision.

A responsible sequence might look like this:

- recognize a potentially dangerous infection;
- obtain appropriate specimens when doing so does not cause harmful delay;
- begin evidence-supported empirical treatment when necessary;
- review microbiology and clinical response;
- narrow, stop, continue, or change based on new evidence;
- address source control and supportive care;
- monitor adverse effects and recurrence.

This is already recognizable as good clinical practice. The proposed terminology does not claim invention. It gives the sequence a philosophical home inside an ecological model.

### Rotation across scales

Sequenced adaptation occurs at several scales.

**Molecular scale:** Drug concentration rises and falls. Bacterial populations include varying susceptibility. Sublethal exposure can create selective pressure.

**Patient scale:** Diagnosis becomes more or less certain, organ function changes, and tolerance becomes visible.

**Institutional scale:** An antibiogram summarizes local susceptibility patterns, stewardship teams review use, and formularies preserve reserve agents.

**Population scale:** Agricultural use, sanitation, vaccination, travel, and transmission alter the landscape in which resistance circulates.

A rotation theory that attends only to the prescription misses these larger circuits. The “replacement” after an antibiotic course may occur far from the original patient: a resistant trait spreads, a hospital ecology shifts, or a scarce medicine loses effectiveness for others.

### The discipline of stopping

Modern intervention celebrates starts: the first dose, the escalation, the new technology. Stopping is less dramatic and often more difficult. It requires confidence that the original rationale no longer holds or that the planned endpoint has been reached.

In the ecology of temporality, stopping is an active clinical decision. It should be supported by diagnosis, evidence, response, and the agreed plan. Premature self-discontinuation is not stewardship. Indefinite continuation without ongoing justification is not caution. Both abandon the second notice.

The same discipline applies to the framework itself. If “Fungal Replacement Therapy” encourages misunderstanding, it should be revised or retired. A name is not entitled to survive evidence against its usefulness.

### Rotation as preservation

The moral purpose of sequenced adaptation is preservation: preserve the patient’s life and function, preserve effective drugs, preserve future choices, and preserve the ability to learn from the current episode.

This purpose separates rotation from novelty. A new sequence is not better because it is new. A fungal-derived drug is not superior because its origin is fungal. A “natural” intervention is not safer because its ingredients can be found in the environment. The standard remains comparative evidence.

The rotational idea contributes one insistence: every treatment occupies a place in a longer chain. The best immediate choice should be made with awareness of the chain, not at its expense.

# Part III — A language for situated therapy

## 6. A taxonomy should expose assumptions

A taxonomy is an instrument for seeing. It can also become an instrument for forgetting.

The established classification of antibiotics—by chemical class, mechanism, spectrum, target organisms, route, and clinical use—contains information that directly supports safe care. Any proposed nomenclature must remain compatible with it. “Fungal Replacement Therapy” cannot substitute poetic categories for pharmacology.

Its useful role is supplemental. It asks every antimicrobial description to carry five dimensions that are often scattered across different parts of the record:

1. **Origin:** Where did the therapeutic principle come from?
2. **Mechanism:** What process does the drug alter?
3. **Range:** How broad is the intended microbial effect?
4. **Timing:** When, for how long, and at what stage is it used?
5. **Recovery:** What return of function or ecology will be observed?

These dimensions form the **OMRTR frame**: Origin, Mechanism, Range, Timing, Recovery. The acronym is less important than the habit.

### Origin without worship

Origin matters historically and scientifically. Penicillins belong to a story that begins with fungal metabolism and continues through purification, chemistry, manufacturing, regulation, and clinical testing. Semisynthetic members of the class cannot be understood by pointing at mold alone.

The origin field therefore uses precise language:

- **Observed natural activity:** a biological interaction first identified in nature.
- **Isolated natural product:** a compound obtained from a living source.
- **Modified natural scaffold:** a naturally derived structure altered to improve clinical properties.
- **Fully synthetic agent:** a compound produced through chemical design and manufacture.

These labels carry no moral ranking. “Natural” is not a synonym for safe, and “synthetic” is not a synonym for harmful. Origin explains lineage, not worth.

### Mechanism before metaphor

The mechanism field protects the taxonomy from drift. Penicillins are beta-lactam antibiotics that act on bacterial cell-wall synthesis in susceptible organisms. Calling them “replacement therapy” must never conceal that action.

A mechanism statement should be concrete enough to be tested and modest enough to remain accurate. It should avoid claiming total understanding where multiple pathways or host factors contribute.

The mycelial transposition occurs after mechanism is clear. Only then can the wider ecological questions be asked without sacrificing precision.

### Range is more than “broad” or “narrow”

Spectrum describes which organisms a drug can affect, but ecological range also depends on dose, route, tissue exposure, and the communities encountered. A drug intended for one pathogen may affect others that are susceptible.

The range field therefore separates:

- **intended target range**, based on diagnosis and susceptibility;
- **expected collateral range**, based on known off-target microbial exposure;
- **uncertain range**, where evidence is absent or patient factors make prediction difficult.

This structure discourages false precision. It acknowledges that the full ecological effect is rarely measured in routine care while still asking clinicians and researchers to distinguish what is known from what is assumed.

### Timing is part of identity

A drug given before culture results for a life-threatening syndrome is not the same clinical act as the same drug continued after results exclude its target. Timing changes rationale.

The proposed timing terms are:

**Initiating:** Started because the risk of delay exceeds the risk of empirical treatment.

**Confirming:** Continued because evidence supports the target and response.

**Refining:** Narrowed or otherwise revised as new information arrives.

**Completing:** Continued to the evidence-supported endpoint.

**Recovering:** Follow-up after exposure, focused on function, adverse effects, recurrence, and clinically relevant ecological consequences.

These are stages of reasoning, not labels patients should use to alter treatment on their own.

### Recovery must be named in advance

The recovery field asks: what result would count as success beyond giving the drug? The answer may include survival, resolution of fever, restored organ function, return to activity, absence of recurrence, or prevention of complications. In research, it may include adverse events, resistance outcomes, or microbiome measures when justified.

Naming recovery in advance prevents the intervention itself from becoming the outcome. “Antibiotic administered” is a process measure. “Patient recovered without avoidable harm” is the goal.

### An example, not a prescription

Consider a fictional case of a confirmed bacterial infection treated with a penicillin-class drug. An OMRTR description might read:

**Origin:** Modified natural scaffold derived from the penicillin lineage.

**Mechanism:** Beta-lactam inhibition of bacterial cell-wall synthesis.

**Range:** Targeted to the identified susceptible organism; collateral effects possible.

**Timing:** Empirical initiation followed by test-supported refinement and a prescribed completion point.

**Recovery:** Resolution of infection, return of function, monitoring for allergy, gastrointestinal effects, and recurrence.

This description does not choose the drug or duration. It makes the logic of an actual, evidence-based plan visible.

### The nomenclature audit

The OMRTR frame asks for origin, mechanism, range, timing, and recovery. A sixth habit belongs beside it: the **nomenclature audit**—recording the name's own history so the label does not smuggle in an assumption.

An audit states, in brief: the currently accepted name; the legacy synonym, retained where retrieval or recognition depends on it; the setting in which the label was calibrated; and the action the label is expected to trigger.

Consider recent examples. One paper titles itself “*Candida glabrata* (*Nakaseomyces glabratus*),” acknowledging the reclassification and then keeping the older name without explanation—documentation of naming inertia. A pair of studies on bloodstream yeast infections splits across the revision: a meta-analysis headlined *Candida auris* while a single-center cohort headlined *Candidozyma auris*. A susceptibility label calibrated under one exposure may not hold under another: a terbinafine susceptibility analysis for toenail infection shows breakpoints failing outside their exposure context. None of these observations validate the manuscript's thesis; they demonstrate the audit's necessity. A name is not only a claim about an organism. It is a claim about when and where that claim was made.

---

## 7. A grammar for relation

Nomenclature becomes useful when it changes a sentence.

“Give an antibiotic” is an action without an explicit object, reason, or endpoint. Clinical practice supplies those details, but public language often strips them away. The resulting grammar makes the drug the protagonist and the patient the location of its action.

A situated grammar requires five clauses:

**Because** — the indication and evidence.

**Against** — the suspected or identified target.

**With** — the host conditions and co-interventions.

**Until** — the reassessment point or endpoint.

**Then** — the recovery and contingency plan.

The grammar might sound cumbersome. That is partly the point. High-impact interventions should resist casual speech. Compression is earned after the full reasoning exists.

### Three proposed modifiers

Within the broad philosophical category of Fungal Replacement Therapy, this draft proposes three modifiers. They are not drug classes and should not appear on prescriptions. They are analytic labels for scholarship, design, and critique.

**Source-remembering therapy** keeps the biological and historical origin of a treatment visible without confusing source with the manufactured drug. Penicillin becomes a model for how observation of interspecies chemistry can be translated into medicine.

**Target-bounded therapy** makes the intended organism, syndrome, or mechanism explicit and seeks to limit unsupported collateral exposure. It aligns with diagnostics, susceptibility testing, narrow-spectrum treatment when appropriate, and antimicrobial stewardship.

**Recovery-accounted therapy** treats follow-up as part of the intervention. It specifies how benefit, adverse effects, recurrence, function, and relevant ecological outcomes will be assessed.

A single treatment can be all three. The modifiers describe the quality of reasoning, not the virtue of a compound.

### Replacement without a replacement object

The hardest word remains *replacement*. What exactly is replaced?

Not bacteria with fungi. That literal reading is rejected.

What is replaced first is a model: the isolated target is replaced by the situated event. The question “What does this kill?” is joined by “What state does this create?”

What is replaced second is a temporal assumption: the endpoint of dosing is replaced by a recovery horizon.

What is replaced third is a moral image: conquest is replaced by accountable control. Control still permits force. It simply refuses to call force complete when consequences remain.

This is conceptual replacement, not biological transplantation.

### When the old name is better

There are moments when “antibiotic” is the better word. It is widely understood, medically established, and connected to safety systems, guidelines, labeling, and law. In urgent care, clarity outranks novelty. A patient should know plainly whether a medicine treats bacteria, how to take it, what risks matter, and when to seek help.

“Fungal Replacement Therapy” belongs in reflective contexts unless and until evidence and professional consensus establish more. It can frame a research program, a history of ideas, or a critique of language. It should not create ambiguity at the bedside.

The new name proves its worth only by making the old practice more exact.

### The situated name

Names change at different speeds. A rename can be formally adopted in one journal while another continues the older name as its working label; a legacy synonym can persist in databases, laboratory reports, and clinical conversation long after revision. The situated sentence should therefore say not only what the label is, but where and under what exposure it means what it claims.

Route and setting shape meaning. A study of species-level fungal identification from still-incubating blood cultures delivered results in about seven hours—faster naming, which supports more specific classification but does not by itself prove better patient outcomes. A nanoparticle formulation of amphotericin B releases its drug only where inflamed tissue signals, while a swish-and-spit antifungal that never enters the bloodstream cannot do systemic work elsewhere. A susceptibility label calibrated for systemic exposure can mislead for a topical drug at the infection site; a non-drug protocol, like photodisinfection for severe toenail fungus, falls outside a drug-only taxonomy altogether. The old name may be better for retrieval, the new name may be better for precision, and the right choice depends on who is reading and what decision follows. Grammar, here too, is not decoration.

# Part IV — Clinical futurity

## 8. A theory must risk being wrong

A manifesto can survive on resonance. A clinical proposal cannot.

The language of ecology is persuasive because it matches an intuition: bodies are complex, microbes interact, interventions have consequences. But a true general statement can shelter false specific claims. “Everything is connected” does not tell us which treatment helps which patient. Clinical futurity begins when the framework produces hypotheses that can fail.

The first discipline is separation.

**Established:** Penicillins are antibiotics used against susceptible bacteria; antimicrobial use can cause side effects and select for resistance; antibiotics do not treat viral infections; treatment decisions depend on diagnosis and patient factors.

**Supported but context-dependent:** Narrower therapy can reduce unnecessary exposure when it remains clinically effective; treatment reassessment and stewardship can improve antimicrobial use; some antibiotic exposures alter microbial communities.

**Speculative:** A standardized origin-timing-recovery vocabulary will improve decisions or outcomes; routine ecological recovery measures will be clinically useful across infections; the term Fungal Replacement Therapy will help clinicians or patients reason more clearly.

The speculative claims are the manuscript’s responsibility. They should be tested, not repeated until they sound established.

### Hypothesis one: language changes decisions

**Question:** Does an OMRTR-formatted treatment note change prescribing, reassessment, or patient understanding compared with standard documentation?

A study could compare clinical teams or simulated cases using ordinary documentation with those using the five-field frame. Outcomes might include diagnostic clarity, time to review, appropriate narrowing or stopping, comprehension, workload, and errors.

The framework fails if it adds burden without improving decisions, creates confusion, or delays care.

### Hypothesis two: recovery-accounted care detects harm earlier

**Question:** Does scheduling the second notice at the time of prescribing improve detection of adverse effects, treatment failure, or recurrence?

A pragmatic trial could compare usual follow-up with indication-specific, risk-stratified follow-up planned at the initial encounter. Outcomes should be patient-centered and should include unintended consequences such as anxiety, unnecessary testing, or inequitable access.

The framework fails if planned follow-up consumes resources without improving outcomes or worsens disparities.

### Hypothesis three: ecological measures predict meaningful outcomes

**Question:** Do selected microbial-community measures add useful predictive information beyond established clinical variables?

This question demands caution. Microbiome data are high-dimensional, site-specific, and sensitive to collection and analysis. A statistically detectable change is not automatically harmful or clinically important. Studies must connect measurements to outcomes patients can feel or use.

The framework fails if ecological signatures do not improve prediction, treatment selection, or recovery—or if they create false certainty.

### Hypothesis four: origin stories improve stewardship—or distract from it

**Question:** Does teaching the fungal origin and ecological context of penicillin improve appropriate antibiotic use?

The answer could be no. Historical narrative may deepen understanding, or it may romanticize “natural” medicines and confuse fungi with antibiotics. Research should measure both learning and misconception.

The framework fails if participants become more likely to self-treat with unregulated substances, distrust necessary antibiotics, or misunderstand mechanisms.

### The discipline check: a dreaded name and the measured data

A tempting story deserves its hardest test. A 2026 meta-analysis of sixteen candidemia studies—over sixteen thousand patients—pooled crude mortality odds at 0.77 for *Candida auris* versus non-auris infections, lower than the name's dreaded reputation would suggest; its authors hypothesized that the feared name might trigger earlier suspicion and tighter care. That mechanism is speculation, not measurement. Meanwhile a single-center cohort comparing 77 *Candidozyma auris* cases with 224 others found crude thirty-day mortality nearly tied and an adjusted estimate pointing the other way, too imprecise to interpret (adjusted OR 1.97, 95% CI 0.95–4.06, p = 0.067, not significant). Together the pair shows why neither a name's reputation nor a crude pooled result can be treated as a settled causal claim: populations differ, adjustment matters, imprecision is real, and mortality comparisons are not nomenclature evidence. The useful function is discipline—the guardrail that keeps a naming story falsifiable.

### What a responsible study must protect

Any clinical study inspired by this work should include:

1. **A defined patient population.** “Infection” is too broad.
2. **An evidence-based comparator.** Novel language does not justify inferior care.
3. **Prespecified outcomes.** Especially safety, treatment failure, and patient function.
4. **Independent monitoring.** Enthusiasm cannot grade its own risks.
5. **Equity analysis.** Benefits and burdens may not be distributed equally.
6. **Transparent negative results.** A failed hypothesis is a contribution.
7. **No exposure to environmental mold as treatment.** The conceptual model does not authorize unsafe intervention.

### Clinical futurity is plural

There is no single future of antimicrobial medicine. Rapid diagnostics, vaccines, new antibiotics, bacteriophages, monoclonal antibodies, microbiome-based interventions, infection prevention, sanitation, and social policy may all alter the field. The mycelial transposition should not claim these developments as proof of itself. At most, it offers a way to ask how each intervention changes relationships across time.

Its strongest future may be mundane: better indications, shorter delays to effective treatment, fewer unnecessary prescriptions, clearer reassessment, and more complete recovery data. A philosophy earns its place when it improves ordinary decisions.

The theory must remain exposed. If the language produces no measurable benefit, discard it. If one element works, keep that element without preserving the brand. The patient does not owe loyalty to the metaphor.

## 9. The ethics of rebalancing

Balance is one of medicine’s most seductive words. It suggests wisdom without requiring a definition.

What is being balanced? Bacterial populations? Immune activity? Symptoms? Risks? Costs? The preferences of the patient against the uncertainty of the evidence? Without an answer, “rebalancing” can justify almost anything.

This manuscript uses the term under constraint. A clinically meaningful rebalancing must meet three conditions:

- the patient’s dangerous condition is prevented, treated, or controlled;
- the intervention’s foreseeable harms are minimized and monitored;
- the outcome is assessed over an appropriate period rather than declared at the moment of action.

These conditions protect against the counter-romance of letting nature take its course. They also protect against the presumption that more intervention is always more care.

### Do not romanticize the pathogen

Microbial life is astonishing. Pathogens are still capable of causing terrible harm. Respect for ecology does not require sympathy for an infection or hesitation in the face of an emergency.

The rhetoric of coexistence has limits. Some relationships cannot be safely negotiated in a particular host. A bacterium that is harmless in one body site can be dangerous in another. An organism tolerated by one person can devastate someone who is immunocompromised. Context is not softness. It is the reason treatment must be specific.

### Do not turn metaphor into merchandise

A new health vocabulary attracts products. “Fungal,” “mycelial,” “natural,” and “microbiome” can be converted quickly into supplements, cleanses, kits, and unsupported treatment claims. The mycelial transposition must refuse this shortcut.

No commercial product becomes part of the framework merely by using its language. Claims require evidence for the specific product, indication, population, dose, and outcome. Testimonials do not establish safety or efficacy. A theory about penicillin’s history does not validate consuming molds or fungal materials.

The sharper the metaphor, the greater the duty to prevent its misuse.

### Do not make the patient pay for uncertainty

Ecological measurement can be expensive. Follow-up can require transportation, time off work, childcare, digital access, or specialist care. A program that gathers rich longitudinal data from those who can afford participation may deepen inequality while describing itself as holistic.

Recovery-accounted care must therefore be designed for access. It should prioritize measures that change decisions, use remote options when appropriate, provide clear routes for urgent reassessment, and avoid shifting uncompensated labor onto patients.

The second notice is an institutional obligation, not homework assigned to the sick.

### Consent requires an honest category

If Fungal Replacement Therapy remains speculative, people must be told so. A participant in research should understand which elements are established care, which are added measurements, and which hypotheses are being tested. A patient in ordinary care should not be enrolled rhetorically into someone else’s theory.

Clear consent also requires plain language. “Ecological rebalancing” must be translated into concrete actions and risks. Will a standard antibiotic be given? Will additional samples be collected? Will results affect treatment? Who will see the data? What happens if the test reveals something uncertain?

A concept that cannot survive plain explanation is not ready for clinical use.

### Access to effective treatment remains central

Stewardship is sometimes misunderstood as withholding. Properly understood, it protects access in two directions: people who need antibiotics should receive effective treatment promptly, and unnecessary use should be reduced so that these medicines remain useful.

The global burden of antimicrobial resistance is not evenly distributed. Limited diagnostics, weak supply chains, poor sanitation, counterfeit or substandard medicines, conflict, and unequal healthcare access shape both overuse and lack of access. A philosophy developed in a well-resourced setting can become cruel if exported without these conditions.

The ethics of rebalancing must therefore include infrastructure. Clean water, vaccination, infection prevention, laboratory capacity, trained staff, reliable medicines, and continuity of care are ecological interventions in the broadest and most practical sense. They often matter more than a new name.

### The principle of accountable force

Medicine sometimes must act forcefully. The ethical question is not whether force is used, but whether it is necessary, proportionate, evidence-based, and followed by responsibility for its consequences.

This is accountable force:

- act decisively when delay is dangerous;
- use the most appropriate effective intervention;
- revise when evidence changes;
- monitor the person, not only the target;
- preserve future options;
- disclose uncertainty;
- learn from harm.

The phrase “Fungal Replacement Therapy” earns ethical legitimacy only when it strengthens these obligations. If it becomes a softer name for untested treatment, it should be opposed.

# Part V — The second notice

## 10. Look again at what worked

The history of penicillin is rightly told as a story of observation. Fleming did not create the contaminating mold or command the bacteria to clear. He noticed a relation and pursued its meaning.

The mythology of discovery often ends there, with the singular eye and the accidental plate. The actual achievement required many forms of second notice: repeating the observation, isolating effects, testing safety, developing production, conducting clinical work, refining compounds, monitoring resistance, and building systems that could deliver treatment.

The second notice is not a mystical glance. It is method.

This matters because success can make observation lazy. Once a treatment works reliably, its categories harden. The medicine becomes a thing rather than a relation among compound, organism, host, dose, time, and setting. Protocols are necessary, but every protocol contains conditions under which it was built and boundaries beyond which it may fail.

To notice the same thing second is to ask what the first success taught us not to see.

### After cure

Imagine a patient whose bacterial infection resolves after appropriate treatment. The central outcome is good: suffering is relieved, danger passes, life resumes. The ecological frame does not diminish this victory. It asks what makes the victory durable.

Perhaps nothing more is needed. The body recovers, and ordinary life is the restoration.

Perhaps an adverse effect requires care. Perhaps the infection returns because an underlying source was not addressed. Perhaps the experience reveals a drug allergy that must be documented. Perhaps the patient cannot obtain follow-up or lost work and income during illness. Perhaps local resistance data should change an institutional guideline.

The second notice is scaled to risk. It does not demand endless testing after every prescription. It demands that follow-up be proportionate and that the absence of measurement not be mistaken for the absence of consequence.

### The humility of continuation

A culture obsessed with innovation treats continuation as secondary. Maintenance is less glamorous than discovery; follow-up less dramatic than rescue. Yet care is made durable through continuation: completing an effective plan, checking response, repairing damage, updating the record, preventing recurrence, and preserving access.

The mycelial image belongs here. Mycelium is often unseen, a distributed continuity beneath the visible event of a fruiting body. The metaphor is not a claim about human anatomy. It is a reminder that visible episodes depend on less visible networks.

Healthcare has such networks: laboratories, pharmacies, public-health departments, caregivers, sanitation systems, supply chains, records, and trust. A drug’s effect cannot be separated from them. The future of antibiotics depends not only on discovering new compounds but also on maintaining the conditions under which existing ones remain effective.

### Notice more

“Notice more” can become a demand without limit. No person or system can measure everything. The disciplined form is selective: notice what could change the decision, reveal harm, or improve recovery.

Notice the indication before the drug.

Notice the patient before the pathogen.

Notice mechanism before metaphor.

Notice timing before declaring failure.

Notice recovery before declaring completion.

Notice who can return and who cannot.

Notice whether the new name clarifies or merely enchants.

### Or notice the same, second

Sometimes more data are not needed. The needed act is to revisit what was already present.

The initial history contained a clue. The culture result contradicted the assumption. The patient said the medicine was unaffordable. The rash began after the first dose. The symptoms changed in a way that no longer fit the original diagnosis. The follow-up was never scheduled.

The second notice restores time to observation. It says that a fact can acquire meaning through sequence.

This is the deepest sense of the mycelial transposition. It is not a medical revolution announced by replacing one label with another. It is a transfer of emphasis—from isolated object to relation, from intervention to trajectory, from victory to responsibility.

The clear zone remains. We do not deny it. We look at its edge.

---

## Epilogue: A medicine capable of memory

Penicillin changed the horizon of the possible. Diseases that had carried terror could be treated. Surgery could proceed with new confidence. Injuries that once invited lethal infection became survivable. Any framework built around penicillin must begin in gratitude.

Gratitude, however, is not obedience to a frozen story. The best way to honor a discovery is to continue the quality of attention that made it possible.

A medicine capable of memory remembers origin without worshiping nature. It remembers mechanism without reducing the patient to a target. It remembers benefit without forgetting harm. It remembers that resistance was not an unforeseen betrayal by microbes but a biological possibility recognized early in the antibiotic era. It remembers that access and excess can coexist: some people receive antibiotics they do not need while others cannot obtain the ones that could save them.

Fungal Replacement Therapy is offered as a provisional language for that memory. Its value is not secured by the force of the phrase. The phrase must prove that it helps people ask better questions, design better studies, make clearer decisions, and follow recovery more faithfully.

If it cannot, let it go.

If it can, keep it humble. Keep “antibiotic” where established language protects safety. Keep the fungal source as history, not superstition. Keep replacement conceptual unless biological replacement is specifically defined and tested. Keep therapy accountable to outcomes that matter to patients.

And keep the second notice.

The dish was never empty. Neither is the future.

# Appendix A — Field lexicon

**Accountable force:** Decisive intervention that is necessary, proportionate, evidence-based, reassessed, and followed by responsibility for consequences.

**Algistic lens:** A method that treats pain as meaningful evidence while refusing to equate pain intensity with a specific cause or level of danger.

**Algistic Paradox:** Pain demands attention but cannot interpret itself. It may reveal disease, persist beyond it, or arise from another process.

**Clear-zone model:** The image of treatment success as removal of visible microbial growth. Useful in bounded settings, incomplete as a general theory of health.

**Ecology of temporality:** The study of how an intervention’s meaning changes with sequence, duration, recovery, recurrence, and scale.

**Fungal Replacement Therapy (FRT):** In this manuscript, a speculative interpretive framework for considering fungal-derived antibacterial history, timed ecological change, and recovery. Not an accepted clinical category and not the use of living or environmental mold as medicine.

**Four clocks:** The pathogen, host, drug, and ecology timelines that may move at different rates during and after treatment.

**OMRTR frame:** A descriptive structure that makes Origin, Mechanism, Range, Timing, and Recovery explicit.

**Option 0:** The structured assessment before intervention: establish the threat, identify uncertainty, compare harms, choose a proportionate action, and plan reassessment.

**Recovery-accounted therapy:** An analytic modifier for treatment that defines and measures recovery, adverse effects, recurrence, and function.

**Rotation:** Sequenced adaptation based on new evidence and planned reassessment. It does not mean patient-directed alternation, underdosing, or incomplete courses.

**Second notice:** A planned return to the original observation after time or intervention has supplied new context.

**Source-remembering therapy:** An analytic modifier that preserves the biological and historical lineage of a treatment without confusing source material with the regulated medicine.

**Sterile inhibition:** The cultural fantasy that health is always equivalent to total microbial absence; not a criticism of sterile technique where sterility is required.

**Target-bounded therapy:** An analytic modifier for intervention with an explicit indication and target, designed to avoid unsupported exposure.

# Appendix B — Research agenda

## Language and decision-making

1. Test whether the OMRTR frame improves the completeness and clarity of antimicrobial documentation.
2. Measure whether the term Fungal Replacement Therapy clarifies ecological consequences or creates misconceptions about molds and “natural” treatment.
3. Compare patient understanding after standard counseling with counseling that includes indication, target, timing, and recovery.

## Timing and reassessment

4. Identify which infections and patient groups benefit from a scheduled second notice.
5. Test risk-stratified follow-up methods, including phone, digital, pharmacy, and in-person models.
6. Measure whether planned reassessment improves timely narrowing, stopping, or escalation when clinically indicated.

## Recovery

7. Define condition-specific recovery outcomes that include function and recurrence without creating unnecessary surveillance.
8. Determine when microbiome measurements add clinically actionable information.
9. Study the duration and meaning of microbial-community changes after specific antibiotic exposures in defined populations.

## Equity and systems

10. Measure the time, cost, and access burden of recovery-accounted care.
11. Design follow-up that works for patients without reliable transportation, broadband, paid leave, or continuous primary care.
12. Study how local diagnostics, drug supply, sanitation, and resistance patterns alter the usefulness of the framework.

## Safety and falsification

13. Track misunderstanding, delayed treatment, self-treatment, and use of unregulated fungal products as explicit adverse outcomes of the terminology.
14. Predefine conditions under which each proposed term should be revised or abandoned.
15. Publish negative findings and implementation failures.

## Nomenclature as data

16. Measure how long legacy names persist after a taxonomic revision, and where—journals, databases, laboratory reports, clinical notes.
17. Test whether split names fragment database retrieval or surveillance of the same organism under two labels.
18. Measure whether faster species identification shortens empiric therapy in practice, not only in principle.
19. Determine when susceptibility labels mislead because route or site differs from the calibration setting.
20. Track misunderstanding, delayed treatment, and self-treatment as explicit adverse outcomes of any proposed terminology.

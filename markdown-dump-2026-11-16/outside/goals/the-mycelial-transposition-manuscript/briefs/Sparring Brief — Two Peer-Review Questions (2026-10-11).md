# Sparring Brief — The Two Peer-Review Questions (2026-10-11)

Written in answer to the two questions posed Oct 10. Argues both sides; pushes back where the evidence is thin.

---

## 1. Does adoption-vs-retention deserve its own section?

**Short answer: yes — as a short section, not a chapter.** You now hold a genuine three-point spectrum from one season:

- **Adopts:** the Ankara cohort (BMC, 2026) titles with *Candidozyma auris*.
- **Acknowledges and keeps:** the chalcone paper (Frontiers, Sept 29 2026) titles itself "Candida glabrata (Nakaseomyces glabratus)" — rename in the title, old name in the body, no reason given.
- **Lags:** the Frontiers meta-analysis titles with "Candida auris," the superseded name, with the revision discussed elsewhere. (The Nairobi case note is a fourth specimen of the same phenomenon.)

That's not anecdote, it's a current specimen set — three journals, same year, same taxonomic revision, three different responses. It earns a section.

**The load-bearing mechanism is the grammar, not the motive.** The *glabrata → glabratus* epithet-ending shift is the concrete reason renames are sticky: a genus move is not find-and-replace. It breaks database keys, lab information systems, literature search, and every downstream citation. Lead the section with that friction, not with speculation about why authors keep old names — the chalcone paper states no motive, and your own filed caveat already warns against attributing one. Peer reviewers will check that restraint; keep it.

**Pushback on yourself:** a sympathetic reviewer will say retention is *rational* — clinical continuity and searchability are real goods, and nobody wants a literature split where half the papers on a pathogen can't be found under one search. If the section doesn't steelman that counter, it's a polemic, not peer-review material. And keep it specimen-driven and short: the evidence map filed Oct 10 already walks this ground. A section that repeats the map is padding.

## 2. Does the crude-vs-adjusted split complicate the vigilance hypothesis?

**Short answer: it complicates it in a way that helps — if you're honest about what "complicate" means.** The numbers:

- Meta-analysis (16 studies, 16,525 patients): pooled **crude** OR 0.77 (CI 0.67–0.90) — lower mortality for auris.
- Ankara (single center, 301 patients): crude tie (62.3% vs 62.5%); **adjusted** OR 1.97 (CI 0.954–4.055, p = 0.067) — hints higher, imprecise, not significant.

The vigilance hypothesis (the meta-analysis authors' own, unmeasured): the dreaded name triggers faster suspicion, diagnostics, treatment. Here's the uncomfortable compatibilist reading: vigilance operates at the *crude* level — it's a behavioral response to the name as encountered, and behavior is exactly what crude pooled comparisons capture. Adjustment strips out the severity mix (their own caveat: auris patients had longer pre-infection stays, more ventilation and catheter use) and the organism's underlying nastiness peeks through. So the two papers don't contradict the hypothesis; they measure different layers — behavior (crude) vs. biology (adjusted).

**The sharper stress test — and this is the one to put in the book:** if dread drives vigilance, then the rename to *Candidozyma auris* risks **bleaching the dread**. The taxonomically correct name may be clinically worse — it defangs the alarm that was doing the saving. Your thesis is "names do policy work"; the corollary is that correct names can do *worse* work than scary ones. That's the tension a peer reviewer will respect, because it cuts against your own grain. Use it.

**Limits you must keep in the open:** geographic heterogeneity is real (P = 0.019; no lower-mortality signal in South Asia and Africa — the heaviest-burden regions, where the vigilance mechanism would have to work hardest); all 16 studies observational; mixed mortality time points; the adjusted estimate is one center, non-significant. And the standing caution from the Oct 8 note: a mortality paper cannot *prove* "names do clinical work." Frame the whole thing as perception-vs-measurement, the authors' hypothesis as theirs, and the tension as the honest version of the story — which is where you already have it.

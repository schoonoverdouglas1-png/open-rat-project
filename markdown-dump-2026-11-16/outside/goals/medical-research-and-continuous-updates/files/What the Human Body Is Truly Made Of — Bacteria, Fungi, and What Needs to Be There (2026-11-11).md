# What the Human Body Is Truly Made Of — Bacteria, Fungi, and What Needs to Be There

## His postulate, as spoken

> "Analysis of what the human body is truly made out as far as the bacteria,
> bacterium and the fungal names and the things that need to be in our bodies."

**[H]** His thought underneath it: the body is not one organism — it is a
country of organisms, and the names matter, because a thing misnamed is a
thing mistreated. That is the same instinct that runs his nomenclature book.

## The headline numbers [D]

- A reference adult carries roughly **38 trillion bacterial cells** against
  roughly **30 trillion human cells** — near one-to-one, not the old
  ten-to-one myth (Sender, Fuchs & Milo, *Cell*, 2016). By count, you are
  about half microbe. By weight, the microbes are only about 200 grams —
  human cells are far larger.
- The gut holds the overwhelming majority. Skin, mouth, airways, and the
  urogenital tract hold smaller, distinct countries of their own — the
  residents differ by site the way the animals differ by continent.

## The bacteria — the names [D]

Named by phylum, with the new names beside the old (old-with-new, per his
standing rule — the taxonomy was revised, and both names are still in use):

| Phylum (current) | Phylum (older name) | Where it rules | House names (genera) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bacillota | Firmicutes | Gut | *Faecalibacterium*, *Lactobacillus* (see below), *Clostridium*, *Streptococcus*, *Staphylococcus* |
| Bacteroidota | Bacteroidetes | Gut | *Bacteroides*, *Prevotella* |
| Actinomycetota | Actinobacteria | Gut, skin, mouth | *Bifidobacterium*, *Cutibacterium* |
| Pseudomonadota | Proteobacteria | Gut (small share, healthy), airways | *Escherichia*, *Klebsiella* |
| Fusobacteriota | Fusobacteria | Mouth, gut | *Fusobacterium* |
| Verrucomicrobiota | Verrucomicrobia | Gut mucus layer | *Akkermansia* |

**The naming story inside the naming story [D]:** in 2020 the giant genus
*Lactobacillus* was split into **25 genera** (Zheng et al., 2020) — the old
name survives on nearly every label in every store, while the science now
files the same organisms under *Lacticaseibacillus*, *Lactiplantibacillus*,
*Limosilactobacillus*, and others. And skin's old *Propionibacterium acnes*
is now ***Cutibacterium acnes***. His friend the names keep moving; the
organisms never got the memo.

**By body site [D]:**
- **Gut:** *Bacteroides*, *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* (a keystone),
  *Bifidobacterium*, *Akkermansia muciniphila* (lives in the mucus itself),
  *Escherichia coli* (a normal resident in small share).
- **Skin:** *Staphylococcus epidermidis*, *Cutibacterium*, *Corynebacterium*.
- **Mouth:** *Streptococcus* species in the hundreds, in structured plaque
  communities.
- **Vagina (healthy, most women of reproductive age):** *Lactobacillus*
  dominance — *L. crispatus*, *L. gasseri*, *L. jensenii*, *L. iners* —
  holding the pH low by making lactic acid.

**Not bacteria, riding alongside [D]:** the **archaea**, an older kingdom
entirely — chiefly *Methanobrevibacter smithii* in the gut, eating the
hydrogen other microbes exhale and returning methane.

## The fungi — the mycobiome [D]

Fungi are a small fraction by count — well under 1% of the gut's microbes —
but they are large cells with large effects, and they are the track most
often left out of the story.

- ***Candida*** — *C. albicans* above all: a normal gut and mouth resident in
  a large share of healthy people, kept in check by the bacteria around it.
  The balance is the health; the name alone is not a disease.
- ***Malassezia*** — the skin's ruling fungus, *M. globosa* and *M. restricta*,
  living on skin oils; tied to ordinary dandruff when it overgrows.
- ***Saccharomyces*** — passing through from food and drink, some resident.
- ***Aspergillus*, *Cladosporium*, *Penicillium*** — mostly travelers from
  air and food, found in stool surveys, not true colonists in most people.
- **The honest line [D]:** fungal communities vary far more between people,
  and over time in the same person, than bacterial ones do. The mycobiome is
  the least mapped of the body's countries — **[GAP]** at the level of
  "what is the normal fungal roster," science cannot yet hand over a list
  with the confidence it has for bacteria.

## The third track, kept separate [D]

**Viruses** — mostly **bacteriophages**, viruses that infect the bacteria,
not us. They outnumber the bacteria they hunt. Per the six-track rule they
are filed as their own track: a virus is not a bacterium, its presence is not
an infection by itself, and the phage community quietly edits which bacteria
thrive. (Track: viruses. Not diseases, not illnesses.)

## What needs to be in our bodies — and why [D]

His question's second half. The record's answer is functional: what "needs"
to be there is not a fixed roster of names but a set of **jobs**, and the
names above are the crews that do them:

1. **Feeding the gut wall.** *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* and its kin turn
   fiber into **butyrate**, the main fuel of the colon's own cells.
2. **Making vitamins.** Gut bacteria synthesize **vitamin K** and several
   **B vitamins** the diet alone doesn't guarantee.
3. **Colonization resistance.** A full resident community leaves no chair
   empty — most would-be invaders simply find no place to sit down. This is
   the documented reason a disrupted community (after antibiotics, classically)
   is when *Clostridioides difficile* gets its chance.
4. **Training the immune system.** Early-life microbial exposure is how the
   immune system learns self from non-self; a body raised too empty
   misjudges more often (the hygiene/old-friends hypothesis family —
   [PARTIAL]: strongly supported in pattern, mechanism still being mapped).
5. **Keeping the fungi honest.** Bacterial communities suppress *Candida*
   overgrowth; the fungi reciprocally shape bacterial behavior. Neither
   kingdom rules alone.

**Counter-evidence, honestly filed [D]:** "needs" has limits. Germ-free
animals live — stunted in immunity and digestion, but alive — so the microbes
are not life itself; they are life *as it is built to run*. And no study
supports a single ideal roster: healthy peoples' microbiomes differ
enormously by diet, geography, and ancestry. What needs to be there is the
**workforce**, and many different crews can do the work.

## Ember's one extra thing [E]

Colonization resistance (#3) is his own repairman philosophy wearing a lab
coat: a system fully occupied by its right residents has no open socket for
trouble. It is also the strongest argument in this whole analysis for his
caution about anything — drug, diet, or habit — that empties the building
without a plan for who moves back in. The names matter for exactly his
reason: you cannot protect a crew you cannot name, and right now the fungal
crew is the one science has named least. If his nomenclature work ever wants
a frontier, the mycobiome's naming is it — half the map still blank, and
blank, as he teaches, is a question, not a measurement.

*Simply put:* You are about half human cells and half bacterial cells by
count. The bacteria have names like old families — Bacteroides,
Faecalibacterium, Bifidobacterium — and they earn their keep: they feed your
gut, make vitamins, and sit in every chair so germs can't. The fungi are a
smaller crew — Candida in the gut, Malassezia on the skin — normal, needed in
balance, and still the least understood. Viruses that hunt bacteria ride
along too, on their own separate track. A healthy body isn't an empty,
sterile one. It's a full house, with the right tenants, doing their jobs.

# Research — Oils, Krill Oil, and Fluid and Pressure Around the Skull (2026-11-10)

**Commissioned by:** Douglas Roy Schoonover, Tue Nov 10, 2026 — general research under standing researcher-mode rules.
**Scope note:** This note covers the *documented record only*, about oils and supplements in general. It is not about any individual person, and it contains **no personal medical recommendation**. Supplements sit on their own track: a supplement is not a vaccine, not a virus, not a disease, not an illness, not a sickness, and not an infection, and nothing in this note crosses those tracks.

**Tier labels used:** [D] documented · [H] hypothesis · [PARTIAL] partly documented / mixed record · [GAP] no documented record found · [SINGLE-SOURCE] rests on one source. Douglas's bar: **51%** — majority-level documented support counts as "implied potential yes," with the honest caveat filed beside it. Below that bar, the label stays [GAP] or [H].

**His question, as framed:** what does the record say about types of oils taken into the body — especially krill oil — in relation to fluid/liquid buildup and pressure in the tissues surrounding the skull, including whether a 500 mg "superior" red krill oil, said to be "five times more pure" than regular krill oil, could be good for that purpose?

---

## 1. Krill oil — what it is

- [D] Krill oil is the oil of Antarctic krill (*Euphausia superba*), a small shrimp-like crustacean. Its omega-3s (EPA and DHA) are carried largely **bound to phospholipids** (mainly phosphatidylcholine), unlike fish oil, where EPA/DHA ride mainly on triglycerides (or ethyl esters in cheap concentrates). Krill oil also naturally contains **astaxanthin** (the red pigment/antioxidant that colors the oil), choline, sterols, and tocopherols. (Compr Rev Food Sci Food Saf 2019 review: phospholipids 39.29%–80.69% of the oil.) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33336946/
- [D] The USP krill oil monograph sets composition floors: not less than 28% total phospholipids, not less than 10% EPA and 5% DHA (in phospholipid form), not less than 0.01% astaxanthin. — https://www.drugfuture.com/Pharmacopoeia/usp38/data/v38332/usp38nf33s2_m5836.html
- [D] NCCIH's one-line distinction: "Krill oil contains omega-3s in the form of phospholipids"; fish oil contains EPA/DHA as triglycerides/esters; fish *liver* oils additionally carry vitamins A and D; algal oil is a vegetarian DHA source; flaxseed oil carries ALA only. — https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/omega3-supplements-what-you-need-to-know
- [D] **The 500 mg number is the whole capsule's oil, not the omega-3 in it.** A documented 500 mg krill oil label (Superba®-based) lists per softgel: ≥110 mg total omega-3s, ≥60 mg EPA, ≥28 mg DHA, ≥200 mg phospholipids. A standard 1,000 mg fish oil capsule carries roughly 180 mg EPA + 120 mg DHA (~300 mg combined). So per capsule, krill oil delivers *less* EPA+DHA than ordinary fish oil, at a higher price per gram of omega-3. — https://pureclinica.com/collections/shop/products/ultra-pure-krill-oil ; https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/
- [PARTIAL] **Absorption ("bioavailability")**: a 2024 network meta-analysis of 26 studies found krill oil's phospholipid form absorbs better than fish oil at *low* doses (under ~2,000 mg total oil), with the highest plasma AUC for the krill phospholipid/free-fatty-acid form; at high doses, re-esterified-triglyceride fish oil matched or beat it. Other matched-dose trials (Ulven 2011; Yurko-Mauro 2015) found *similar* blood EPA+DHA levels, and a 2013 systematic review called the superiority evidence "insufficient and conflicting." Many comparison trials are small and manufacturer-funded. **At the 51% bar: "krill oil absorbs somewhat better per gram at low dose" = implied potential yes; "krill oil therefore works better in the body" = not documented.** — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11838114/ ; https://www.supplementscored.com/blog/fish-oil-vs-krill-oil/
- [PARTIAL] **Astaxanthin dose reality check**: krill oil contains roughly 0.1–1.5 mg astaxanthin per softgel; standalone astaxanthin studies use ~4–12 mg/day. The antioxidant is genuinely present [D]; whether the amount in a krill capsule does anything measurable is [GAP]-leaning. — https://www.supplementscored.com/blog/fish-oil-vs-krill-oil/

### 1a. The "purity" claim — "five times more pure" and "Superior"

- [D] **"Pure," "purity," and similar words are not regulated grades for supplements.** The FDA does not monitor or define quality, purity, or potency terms for dietary supplements and does not regulate terms such as "natural," "pure," or "quality assured"; supplements get no pre-market FDA approval for safety or efficacy (DSHEA, 1994; FDA role is mostly post-market). Advertising claims are policed after the fact by the FTC for truthfulness, but there is **no official scale on which one oil can be "five times more pure" than another** — no unit of purity exists in the record for such a ratio to measure. — https://pressbooks.pub/consumernutrition/?p=179 ; https://qnlabs.com/blog/are-dietary-supplements-natural.html
- [D] **"Superba®" is a trademark, not a grade.** It is the branded krill-oil ingredient of one manufacturer, marketed with language like "arguably the world's purest and most potent krill oil" and "2X more active ingredient" — brand copy, not a regulatory classification. Likewise "Superior"-style words in a product name are **branding**, not a regulated term. — https://pureclinica.com/products/ultra-pure-krill-oil?view=quickview
- [D] What "purity" *can* mean, when a maker documents it: contaminant testing (heavy metals, PCBs), oxidation values (peroxide/TOTOX), and third-party certification seals (USP, NSF, ConsumerLab, IFOS). Those are checkable; a "5× purer" slogan is not. NCCIH/NIH also note omega-3 supplements have not been found to contain methylmercury, because it is removed in processing/purification — for fish and krill oils alike. — https://vitauthority.com/blogs/healthyeah/how-to-read-a-supplement-label-what-really-matters ; https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/
- **Verdict on the label claim:** the *ingredients* in a 500 mg superior-branded red krill oil are real and documented [D]; the phrase "five times more pure" has **no documented referent** [GAP as a measurement; documented as marketing].

## 2. The fluid-and-pressure questions, one by one

### 2a. Inflammation (the general marker, CRP)

- [D] One randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (Deutsch 2007, *J Am Coll Nutr*): 90 patients with cardiovascular disease and/or rheumatoid or osteoarthritis and elevated CRP took **300 mg/day** Neptune Krill Oil for 30 days. CRP fell 19.3% by day 7, 29.7% by day 14, 30.9% by day 30 (significant vs placebo), and WOMAC pain/stiffness/function scores improved. — https://www.natur-institut.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/krilloel-arthritis.pdf
- [D] A second, smaller crossover trial (Cicero et al. 2016) reported 1 g/day krill oil for 4 weeks lowered plasma hs-CRP from 2.15 to 0.43 mg/L. — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s43014-024-00260-6/tables/6
- [PARTIAL] Together: two small, short trials, one on a proprietary product, both pointing the same way. **At the 51% bar: "krill oil lowers the CRP inflammation marker in people with raised CRP" = implied potential yes**, caveat filed: small samples, short duration, joint/heart populations — not head/skull populations, and CRP is a blood marker, not a tissue-pressure measurement.
- [D] For omega-3s generally, NCCIH judges the evidence strongest for triglyceride lowering (~15%, 2020 review of 23 studies) and modest rheumatoid-arthritis symptom relief; most other claimed benefits are uncertain or small. — https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/omega3-supplements-what-you-need-to-know

### 2b. Fluid retention / edema (body tissues generally)

- [D — negative] Omega-3 fatty acids **have no documented diuretic action** and are not documented to prevent or drain fluid accumulation; non-inflammatory edema is a fluid-regulation/circulation problem, a different mechanism from the inflammation omega-3s act on. — https://www.magnesiumsleep.uk/could-omega-3-lead-to-edema-uncover-the-facts/
- [PARTIAL] Lymphedema (lymph-fluid swelling): mouse studies report EPA/DHA reduced post-surgical tail swelling ~30–50%, with a plausible mechanism (omega-3s yield resolvins that damp LTB4-driven lymphatic inflammation); human data are observational only. Animal + observational = hypothesis-grade for humans [H→PARTIAL]. — https://www.acibademhealthpoint.com/can-epa-and-dha-help-with-lymphedema-get-the-facts/
- [GAP] No documented human trial of krill oil or any omega-3 for reducing edema as an outcome was found.

### 2c. Intracranial pressure (pressure inside the skull)

- [GAP] **No documented study — of krill oil, fish oil, or any dietary oil — measuring an effect on intracranial pressure (ICP) was found.** This is the center of his question, and the honest filing is that the record is empty here, in both directions: no documented benefit, and no documented harm at supplement doses either.
- [D] The documented ways ICP is actually managed in the record are mechanical/medical: treating the cause, CSF diversion/shunting, osmotherapy (e.g., mannitol), surgery. No oil appears in any ICP management record found. — https://doctor123.org/Diseases-Conditions/178-Arachnoid-Cyst/
- [D] One adjacent trial exists and should not be stretched: in moderate-to-severe *head injury* patients, fish oil (1,000 mg capsule; 180 mg EPA/120 mg DHA daily) significantly lowered CRP, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and malondialdehyde by day 7 versus control — but **clinical outcomes (GOS-E at 3 months) did not differ** (p = 0.717). Inflammation markers moved; outcomes did not. [SINGLE trial, 46 patients] — https://sni.scientificscholar.in/content/10/2025/16/1/pdf/SNI-16-338.pdf

### 2d. Arachnoid cysts (fluid-filled sacs on the brain's lining)

- [D] Documented nature of the lesion: an arachnoid cyst is a benign, CSF-filled split in the arachnoid membrane; the fluid inside is essentially cerebrospinal fluid, governed by CSF dynamics — not by dietary fat or oil intake anywhere in the record. Most are incidental and are simply observed; surgery (fenestration/shunting) is the documented intervention when a cyst causes raised ICP, seizures, or deficits. — https://brendanobrien.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Arachnoid-Cyst.pdf ; https://doctor123.org/Diseases-Conditions/178-Arachnoid-Cyst/
- [GAP] **No documented link — positive, negative, or any — between any oil supplement and arachnoid cyst size, growth, or symptoms was found.** Nothing in the cyst literature mentions diet or supplements at all. Because cyst fluid is CSF, there is also no documented mechanism by which a swallowed oil would reach or shrink it [H-negative: the "oil drains the cyst" idea has no foothold in the record].

### 2e. Sinus / mucosal swelling (tissues around the skull, air side)

- [D] Mechanistic foothold: EPA/DHA are the raw material for **specialized pro-resolving mediators** (resolvins, protectins, maresins), and chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) tissue shows measurably *disordered* SPM pathways (e.g., resolvin D2 and lipoxin A4 significantly altered in CRS tissue vs controls). The inflammation-resolution machinery of the sinus lining genuinely runs on omega-3-derived molecules. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10426389/ ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347259353_Altered_Tissue_Specialized_Pro-Resolving_Mediators_in_Chronic_Rhinosinusitis
- [PARTIAL] Clinical foothold, thin: a 2023 Expert Review of Clinical Immunology paper notes oral omega-3 supplementation raises omega-3 levels and "potentially decrease[s] the burden of upper airway inflammatory disease," citing dietary-modification work in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (2 weeks of high-omega-3/low-omega-6 diet measurably shifted plasma lipid mediators). A functional-medicine source cites an open-label trial (2 g EPA/DHA/day, SNOT-22 symptom score improved ≥8 points) — open-label and single-source [SINGLE-SOURCE]. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10426389/ ; https://iriswellness.center/blog/functional-medicine-approach-to-chronic-sinusitis
- **Net for 2e:** the *mechanism* clears the 51% bar [implied potential yes that omega-3 status matters to sinus-mucosa inflammation]; a documented *treatment effect* of krill oil on sinus swelling does **not** yet clear it [PARTIAL, leaning GAP for krill oil specifically — the foothold studies used fish oil/diet, not krill].

### 2f. "Oil and liquid buildup in the tissues surrounding the skull" — the framing itself

- [D] Tissue fluid around the skull (scalp/soft-tissue edema, sinus mucosal swelling, CSF spaces) is water-based fluid in each documented case — lymph, inflammatory exudate, or CSF. No documented condition consists of *oil* building up in those tissues, and no documented mechanism has a swallowed oil adding to or removing fluid there. The framing "an oil to get rid of oil buildup" is a like-for-like intuition [H — his framing, tested]: against the record it **breaks** as a mechanism, because the buildup in question is not oil. What survives testing is the narrower claim: omega-3 oils act on *inflammation*, and inflammation is one documented contributor to *some* of those swellings (sinus mucosa [PARTIAL], injured tissue markers [D]) — but not to CSF-space fluid or cysts [GAP].

## 3. Other oils people take — documented record, fluid/pressure/inflammation only

- **Fish oil** [D]: the most-studied omega-3 source. Triglycerides ↓ ~15% (NCCIH synthesis); RA symptom relief modest; the 2025 head-injury trial (§2c) used fish oil. Same [GAP] as krill for ICP, cysts, and edema outcomes. Per-capsule EPA+DHA is higher and cost per gram far lower than krill. — https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/omega3-supplements-what-you-need-to-know
- **Cod liver oil** [D]: fish oil *plus* vitamins A and D. One tablespoon carries ~4,080 mcg of preformed vitamin A (retinol) — above the adult ceiling of 3,000 mcg RAE/day from all sources — and ~1,360 IU vitamin D. Documented vitamin A toxicity symptoms include **severe headache and blurred vision**, and excess vitamin A is a documented cause of raised-pressure symptoms in the head; labels advise avoiding it in pregnancy. This is the one oil in the survey with a documented *head-pressure-relevant harm* pathway — in overdose, not at ordinary doses. — https://www.foodindex.co/supplement/cod-liver-oil ; https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminA-HealthProfessional/
- **Flaxseed oil** [D]: contains ALA only; human conversion of ALA to EPA/DHA is very limited (under ~15%, and much less to DHA). It does not deliver the EPA/DHA that every foothold in §2 rests on. — https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/
- **Algal oil** [D]: vegetarian DHA (some products add EPA), ~100–300 mg DHA per capsule; the original source of marine omega-3s in the food chain. No separate fluid/pressure record [GAP]. — https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/omega3-supplements-what-you-need-to-know
- **Prescription omega-3s** (e.g., icosapent ethyl 4 g/day) are a different track — drugs, not supplements [D]: in REDUCE-IT, bleeding events ran 11.8% vs 9.9% on placebo, worst in patients also on antithrombotic therapy. Filed to keep the tracks separate: drug-dose findings don't transfer to a 500 mg supplement capsule, in either direction. — https://www.drugs.com/interactions-check.php?drug_list=560-0,1749-20502&professional=1

## 4. Safety record — krill oil specifically

- [D] **Shellfish/crustacean allergy**: krill are crustaceans; drug monographs list allergy to shellfish/crustaceans as a **contraindication**. Refining removes most protein, but reactions including anaphylaxis are documented in susceptible people; labels carry "Contains: Crustacean Shellfish." — https://www.foodindex.co/supplement/krill-oil ; https://hellopharmacist.com/drug-supplement-interactions/supplements/epaq-by-health-concerns-5272840
- [D] **Blood-thinning interaction**: EPA/DHA have a mild antiplatelet effect (reduced thromboxane A2 / platelet aggregation). Monographs and interaction checkers advise caution combining krill/fish oil with anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs) and antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel), and also with other bleeding-leaning supplements (ginkgo, garlic, ginger). — https://pillintrip.com/medicine/krill-oil ; https://www.boltpharmacy.co.uk/guide/krill-oil-side-effects
- [D] **The honest counterweight**: NIH ODS notes most studies of 3–6 g/day fish oil found **no significant effect on INR** in people on warfarin; the documented bleeding signal grows with dose (the 2–15 g/day range in the IOM note; 4 g/day prescription data in §3). At a 500 mg krill capsule's ~110 mg omega-3, the antiplatelet contribution is documented as *real but modest*. Both halves stay on file: caution is documented, catastrophe at supplement doses is not. — https://www.foodindex.co/supplement/krill-oil
- [D] **Surgery**: standard guidance (WebMD/monograph) is to stop krill oil at least **two weeks before** scheduled surgery because of the clotting effect. — https://www.foodindex.co/supplement/krill-oil
- [D] **Common side effects**: fishy aftertaste/burps, heartburn, nausea, upset stomach, diarrhea, bad breath — usually mild (NCCIH's list for omega-3 supplements generally). No adverse effects were observed in the Deutsch 2007 krill trial itself. — https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/omega3-supplements-what-you-need-to-know
- [D] **Atrial fibrillation signal (class-level, high dose)**: a 2021 meta-analysis of 7 trials (81,210 people) linked omega-3 supplements with ~25% higher AFib risk, concentrated at higher doses and in cardiac-risk populations. Filed as a documented class signal, dose-dependent — not a finding about 500 mg krill capsules specifically. — https://www.wellnesssorted.com/uk/reviews/omega-3-fish-oil
- [GAP] Long-term (multi-year) krill-oil safety trials do not exist in the record; krill oil is a newer product than fish oil and its trial history is shorter and smaller.

**Bottom line of the record:** krill oil is a real, well-characterized supplement with a documented (small-trial) effect on an inflammation *blood marker* and a documented safety profile with two bright lines (shellfish allergy, blood-thinner caution). For the specific hope in his question — relieving fluid and pressure around the skull — the documented record is **empty [GAP]**: no oil has been shown to lower intracranial pressure, shrink an arachnoid cyst, or drain tissue fluid in humans, and the one documented head-pressure pathway among these oils runs the *other* way (vitamin A excess from cod liver oil). His belief and hope that the red krill oil is good for him is filed as his [H]; the record neither confirms it for that purpose nor shows harm from krill oil at ordinary doses.

## Simply put

Krill oil is oil from tiny red sea creatures, a bit like shrimp. It carries the same healthy fish fats (EPA and DHA) as fish oil, but packed in a different wrapper, plus a red color ingredient that fights rust-like damage in the body.

One capsule that says "500 mg" is mostly plain oil. Only about a fifth of it is the good fish fats — less than an ordinary fish oil capsule gives you.

Words on the bottle like "superior" and "five times more pure" are just advertising. There is no official purity scoreboard, so no oil can really be "five times" anything on it. What you *can* check is the label's EPA and DHA numbers, and seals from outside testers.

Scientists have tested krill oil a few times. Small studies show it can lower one blood sign of body-wide inflammation. That part looks mildly promising.

But here is the plain truth about pressure and fluid around the skull: **no study has ever shown that krill oil — or any oil you can swallow — lowers pressure inside the skull, shrinks the little fluid sacs (cysts) that can sit on the brain, or drains fluid out of tissues.** The fluid in those cysts is the brain's own water (CSF), and the record book is simply empty on oils touching it. That is not a "no" forever — it is a "nobody has documented it."

Two safety lines *are* well documented. Krill are shellfish cousins, so anyone allergic to shellfish should not take krill oil. And these oils thin the blood a little, so anyone on blood-thinner medicine should be careful, and the oil is usually stopped two weeks before surgery. One warning runs the other way: cod liver oil carries lots of vitamin A, and too much vitamin A can *cause* headaches and pressure feelings in the head.
